Alibaba (Qwen)

Verifiable Hidden Dynamics Play: Generating Agentic RL Environments from Solved Mechanisms

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-23 Agentic RL · Environment generation · Verifiable rewards · Function calling · Tool use

Overview

  • Xinjie Shen (Georgia Tech), Wei Fan, Xudong Guo, Jianhong Tu, Yang Su, Chuqiao Kuang, Yinger Zhang, Lianghao Deng, Dayiheng Liu — the last eight authors at Alibaba Token Foundry (the Qwen post-training organization). arXiv:2609.27321v1 [cs.LG], submitted 2026-09-23. Qwen affiliation on the majority of authors qualifies the paper under the frontier-labs filter.
  • The problem is that agentic RL training pipelines need diverse, verifiable and cheap environments where success is defined not by rubric-graded LLM judges but by a computable oracle. Existing pipelines build the environment first, then bolt on a reward function — which either loses information or requires domain-expert authoring per task. The paper reverses the dependency: sample a mathematical model of a real optimization problem, solve it to obtain the reference solution and default baseline, and only then generate the interactive environment around that already-computed answer.
  • Contribution: mechanism-first construction. The reference solution fixes the outcome rule before the environment is realized, and an LM setter renders the decision process as a set of stateful tools plus a scenario narrative — producing environments whose rewards are both verifiable (against the pre-computed oracle) and information-asymmetric (parameters θ are withheld from the agent even though the solver used them). The paper releases 3,300 admitted environments spanning 11 mechanism families at $0.01–$0.03 per environment; training Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on them raises its mean agentic score across five optimization families from 0.204 to 0.815.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Mechanism-first construction order.
    • Problem. Conventional agentic-environment pipelines write a scenario, ship it, and then post-hoc author a reward. When the reward is graded by an LLM judge, it is neither faithful nor cheap; when the reward is a hand-authored program, the environment must be constrained to what the author can verify, and diversity collapses. Neither path scales to the hundreds-of-thousands of environments RL agent training needs, and neither guarantees that the reward signal actually measures optimal play.
    • Mechanism. The pipeline runs in this order: (1) sample parameters θ ~ P_f from a mechanism-family distribution f (e.g. inventory dynamic programming, knapsack, linear program, quadratic program, vehicle routing — the paper's five diagnostic families, plus six more for scale); (2) invoke a family-specific solver S_f to compute z_θ = (u*(θ), u_0(θ)), i.e. the optimal utility under the true dynamics and a default-policy baseline; (3) fix the outcome rule ℛ_θ from that solved reference before any environment realization; (4) realize an executable operational form D(θ) from the mathematical model M(θ), converting parameters into state-transition dynamics Λ_θ and a utility functional U_θ; (5) hand the full draw (θ, S_f, M(θ), D(θ)) plus a passage retrieved from a real-world document corpus to a frozen LM setter, which emits E(θ): a scenario narrative, database schema, tool implementations wrapping Λ_θ, and player instructions; (6) wrap the realized dynamics with an observation map Ω_θ and action set 𝒜 that withhold θ from the policy while exposing only environment-facing tool calls.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Pre-solving pins verifiability: reward is scored against u*(θ) not against an LLM judge, and the corpus-grounded setter cannot change the answer because it is downstream of the solver. Deriving the environment from an already-solved problem also lets the pipeline admit only draws whose interactive form remains solvable under a fixed action budget, so the resulting corpus is guaranteed learnable rather than degenerate. Compared to Agent-RLVR (Deng et al., 2606.11425), which grafts programmatic rewards onto existing coding environments, VHD-Play generates the environment and the reward jointly from the same pre-solved problem, and its cost scales with the setter's LLM cost, not with human authoring — the reported $0.01–$0.03 per environment.
  • Named contribution — Information-asymmetric interface.
    • Problem. If the parameters θ that the solver used are exposed to the agent, the agent can in principle re-derive u*(θ) analytically and skip the entire agentic loop; the environment collapses into a math benchmark. But if θ is hidden and the environment offers only tool calls that read partial state, the agent has to interact to discover the same reference solution.
    • Mechanism. The observation map Ω_θ exposes only local, tool-reachable projections of the state; parameters remain in the setter's scope but never appear in observation tokens. Actions 𝒜 are function-calling tool invocations against the setter-authored stateful tools; the agent sees only tool responses. The paper distinguishes three matched conditions on the same underlying θ: a written-out non-agentic prompt (F, the parameters and dynamics fully in the prompt), an informed-agentic environment (I, parameters revealed but interaction still tool-based), and the true agentic setting (A, parameters withheld).
    • Why. The three-condition split is what lets the paper attribute training gains to stateful interaction rather than to problem knowledge. Base Qwen3.6-35B-A3B scores 0.962 in written-out mode — the underlying math is well within its capability — but only 0.231 in the informed-agentic condition and 0.204 in the true agentic condition. The training gap is not knowing the answer, it is knowing how to interrogate the environment to reach one.

Ablations

  • Where does the gain come from — parameters or interaction? The three-condition split (Table 2 above) shows base Qwen3.6-35B-A3B almost solves the math when parameters are in the prompt (0.962 macro) but not when it has to interact (0.204). VHD-Play training closes 84% of the parameter-revealed stateful gap (informed-agentic 0.231 → 0.875) and 77% of the full written-out-to-agentic gap (0.204 → 0.815 against the 0.992 post-training written-out ceiling).
  • Is the gain just teaching the model to call more tools? Within fixed tool-adoption strata across eight families the training gain is +0.413, and only +0.030 of the total gain is attributable to increased tool adoption — the remaining ~94% is quality of stateful interaction, not frequency of it.
  • Policy drift. KL divergence between trained and base policies on held-out trajectory states is D_KL(π_trained || π_base) = 0.089 nats per assistant token: the training moves the policy meaningfully but does not collapse it toward a narrow distribution.
  • Co-scaling on Knapsack (Figure 5 in the paper). Environment difficulty scales cleanly with problem size: 6×5 configurations yield +0.412 gain, 8×7 +0.551, 9×9 +0.675, 11×11 +0.861 — larger problems admit larger, more informative reward signals rather than saturating.

Other

  • The setter LM is frozen during environment generation and is not further trained; the only trained parameters are the RL policy on the generated environments. This isolates the environment-generation cost from any policy-side compute and lets the same setter run against future base models unchanged.
  • Failure modes acknowledged: on TravelBench the trained model still trails Qwen3.7-Max by ~0.1, indicating that transfer from mathematically-solved mechanisms to unstructured planning tasks is partial; on Routing (the family with the least clean closed-form solver) the informed-agentic gap remains ~0.08 after training. Both are consistent with the pipeline's core assumption that verifiable outcome signal is bounded by solver quality.

Shanghai AI Laboratory

InternW0: A Foundational Physical World Model for Efficient Real-World Interactions

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-23 Physical world model · VLA · Flow matching · Asynchronous inference · Wet-lab robotics

Overview

  • Jisong Cai, Yao Mu, Ganlin Yang, Zhe Cao, Zhangzheng Tu, Xing Gao, Kailin Li, Xinyu Zhan, Lixin Yang, Yangkun Zhu, Haoxiang Ma, Ming Zhou, Qiaojun Yu, Yufei Xue, Liqun He, Yifei Yao, Yifan Zhu, Long Ling, Bingqi Jiang, Haoyu Guo, Xueyue Zhu, Bowen Zhou, Bin Zhao, Tianfan Xue, Chunhua Shen, Weinan Zhang. arXiv:2609.27656v1 [cs.RO / cs.LG], submitted 2026-09-23. Physical Intelligence Team, Shanghai AI Laboratory. Project page: internrobotics.github.io/internw0; code at InternRobotics/internw0.
  • The framing is a physical world model whose value is measured not by pixel-space video prediction quality but by whether its predictions remain actionable as the real world changes during a policy step. Prior video-and-action world models (Fast-WAM, single-timescale VLA backbones) tie video prediction and action decoding to the same forward pass, forcing action latency to grow with prediction horizon and giving up on the observation that in most manipulation the action rate has to be an order of magnitude faster than useful video rollouts.
  • Contribution: an asymmetric video–action architecture where a high-capacity video expert runs at long horizons and a lightweight action expert runs at the control rate, coupled by observation-conditioned context routing that reuses the layerwise K/V of the video expert across action steps rather than regenerating it. Plus EgoLab, a 275-hour real-lab egocentric dataset built specifically for wet-lab science tasks, contributing to a full training set of 7,233.5 hours across 25 domains and 811,969 episodes. Real-robot deployment covers a 15-stage metal-organic-framework synthesis workflow and a 5-stage quantitative-pipetting task on top of standard LIBERO / RoboTwin benchmarks.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Asymmetric video–action architecture.
    • Problem. A single-tower video-language-action model has to serve two objectives at odds with each other. Rolling out a useful video prediction wants a large model, a long context, and enough compute per step that inference latency is measured in hundreds of milliseconds. Closed-loop dexterous control wants latency in tens of milliseconds and update rates well over 10 Hz. Prior single-tower VLAs and video-then-action models (Fast-WAM among them) either sacrifice update rate or truncate the prediction horizon. Neither is a true world model in the sense that the prediction remains valid across a control step.
    • Mechanism. InternW0 splits the transformer stack into a video expert and an action expert, sharing tokenization and low-level embeddings but running at different frequencies with different parameter counts. The video expert produces a rollout of future visual state at a slow rate; the action expert consumes the current observation, the current point in the video-expert rollout, and its own recent history to decode a chunk of action tokens at the control rate. Training uses flow matching on both video and action targets under a mixture-of-transformers backbone. Domain-specific interface tokens and soft prompts (learned per embodiment) let the same trunk drive heterogeneous robots without a separate policy head per platform. Contact-aware post-training adds a supervised term over force and tactile signals for tasks where compliance matters.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The split respects the two objectives' natural timescales rather than forcing them onto one. Because the video expert can be scaled without pushing action latency up, the paper is able to run a larger video backbone than a monolithic VLA of the same total budget would allow, and still hit 16.47 Hz model-side update rate at 60.73 ms per action on an RTX 5090D — 3.13× the Fast-WAM baseline's throughput at matched context. Compared to interleaved video-then-action architectures, the axis of improvement is that action decoding does not stall on video rollout.
  • Named contribution — Observation-conditioned context routing.
    • Problem. Even with a split-tower design, if every action step reruns the video expert from scratch on the current observation, the video expert becomes an amortization bottleneck: the same rollout is recomputed for every micro-step of control, with each recomputation mostly duplicating the previous one. This is what caps prior asynchronous designs and is what fixes the effective planning horizon at a small multiple of one video forward pass.
    • Mechanism. Between video rollouts, InternW0 keeps the video expert's layerwise K/V cache alive and routes it through a learned observation-conditioned gate. When a new observation arrives, the gate emits a per-layer transformation of the cached K/V so that the video expert's attention over the ongoing rollout reflects the newly observed state without recomputing the full rollout. The action expert reads through this rerouted cache at every control step. In effect, the video expert runs asynchronously, at its native slow rate, and the action expert benefits from a partial-refresh mechanism whose cost is a small fraction of a fresh forward pass.
    • Why. The refresh cost is bounded by the size of the K/V update (a per-layer routed transformation) rather than a re-embed of the entire context. This is what turns "video-then-action" from a batch two-step into a genuinely asynchronous control loop. The efficiency chart in the paper attributes the 3.13× throughput gain over Fast-WAM primarily to this K/V reuse rather than to raw arithmetic-density improvements in the action expert.
  • Named contribution — EgoLab dataset and contact-aware post-training.
    • Problem. Wet-lab tasks — instrument handling, liquid transfer, mixing, MoF synthesis — are absent from the standard robot-manipulation datasets that VLAs are pretrained on. A world model trained without this footage sees force-sensitive dexterous manipulation only in synthetic simulation, and its post-training has no in-domain contact prior.
    • Mechanism. EgoLab is 275.4 hours of new egocentric video recorded in a real chemistry laboratory covering instrument handling, liquid transfer, mixing, and other wet-lab activity. It contributes to a training pool of 7,233.5 hours across 25 domains and 811,969 episodes, spanning six robot datasets plus egocentric video. Contact-aware post-training adds a supervised loss on force/tactile signals for the pipetting and MoF-synthesis tasks specifically, coupling the action expert to the force channel at the point where the standard visual observation stops being informative.
    • Why. The dataset choice is the key departure from prior physical-world-model work that indexes on simulation and object-manipulation datasets rather than wet-lab footage; the contact-aware term is what makes the model's real-world scores on MoF synthesis and pipetting jump substantially over the visual-only ablation (68.4% vs π0.5's 50.2% progress on MoF; 65.3% vs 46.7% on pipetting).

Ablations

  • Removing observation-conditioned K/V routing (falling back to full-recomputation asynchronous scheduling) drops throughput to roughly the Fast-WAM baseline while leaving in-simulation success largely intact — consistent with the throughput gain being an inference-side property rather than a modeling-quality property.
  • Removing contact-aware post-training substantially degrades MoF-synthesis and pipetting scores while leaving Make-Sandwich and Sort-Tubes near-unchanged, isolating the force/tactile term to tasks where compliance is on the critical path.
  • Training without the EgoLab footage keeps LIBERO / RoboTwin scores nearly at published levels but drops MoF-synthesis and pipetting sharply — the wet-lab pretraining is what carries the scientific-task numbers.

Other

  • Positioning: "the first instantiation of the InternW physical world model series" — naming implies a successor family, and the design (mixture-of-transformers with split experts, flow matching, K/V routing) reads as a template rather than a one-off. The project repository ships the model page infrastructure but the model weights themselves are staged for release rather than posted alongside the paper at v1.
  • Limitations acknowledged: real-world scores on MoF and pipetting are progress scores, not full-success rates, and the 15-trial sample size on each task makes the confidence interval wide; the paper does not report seed variance. On the RoboTwin Clean2Random split the number (75.60%) trails the paper's own RoboTwin-Full number by a substantial margin, indicating the model is not yet robust to strong clutter randomization.
