Qwen-Planner-Agent: A Closed-Loop AI-for-AI Framework for Real-World Mobile Planner Agents

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-24 Agentic RL · Mobile agents · Post-training · Tool use · Memory

Overview

  • Tingyu Qu, Weigao Sun, Yuecheng Liu, Yucheng Zhao, Yi Zhu, Yifeng Ding, Qiyi Wang, Sihan Cao, Pengkun Jiao, Hanlei Xie, Xiongwei Wu, Qichao Wang, Haodong Zhang, Jiajun Liu, Yuhao Wang, Yuqing Xie, Junpeng Zhao, Long Chen, Ming Ma, Sihan Yang, Ziwang Zhao, Yanhao Jia, Liangquan Gong, Feida Zhu, Yiran Zhong and Steven Hoi — Alibaba Tongyi-MAI (the Mobile-AI subgroup of Alibaba's Tongyi Lab / Token Hub). arXiv:2609.29892v1 [cs.AI], submitted 2026-09-24, on the Hugging Face Daily selection for 2026-09-25. The author list overlaps heavily with the MobilePA-Bench paper (Alibaba Tongyi-MAI, arXiv:2608.23035, covered 2026-08-26), positioning Qwen-Planner-Agent as the trained-model companion to that August-shipped benchmark.
  • The problem the paper takes on is that mobile-planner training pipelines have three partially-decoupled leaks: training data that never sees deployment errors, a post-training reward whose scale does not track the model's current competence (either too generous and the RL never finds a signal, or too strict and the model collapses to short trajectories), and a runtime harness whose retrieved skills and memories are context-adapted only in the sense of last-turn prompt injection. The paper closes all three under a single closed loop.
  • Contribution: a three-stage AI-for-AI lifecycle (data / training / harness) unified by the same underlying Qwen-Planner-Agent policy. Trained at 27B total parameters, the agent reaches 77.05% Overall on MobilePA-Bench, +9.83 points over its own Qwen3.8-27B base and ahead of GPT-6 Astra (76.84%) and Claude Opus 5 (75.71%), at an estimated $2.41 per 1,000 tasks in output-token cost (including reasoning tokens; input tokens and infrastructure excluded).

Methodology

  • Named contribution — The AI-for-AI closed loop.
    • Problem. A conventional agent pipeline has three disconnected stages: humans write the training data, humans write the reward, humans write the retrieval prompts. Each stage sees a static caricature of the others: the training data doesn't see runtime failures, the reward doesn't see the current model's competence curve, and the harness doesn't see which skills the model has actually internalised. When the deployed model logs a failure, the failure has to be manually attributed to one of the three, then propagated by hand.
    • Mechanism. Three specialised agents replace the three human loops. AI for Data: a task-construction agent generates realistic mobile-planning tasks against the MobilePA-Bench sandbox and collects interaction trajectories, then an evidence-preserving curation agent balances the resulting corpus by coverage of tool families, memory patterns, skill invocations and sub-agent dispatch. AI for Training: SFT on the curated trajectories is followed by hybrid-environment RL under CARE (see below). AI for Harness: deployed agent traces are consumed by a runtime Scenario Adapter that adapts which skills, memories and sub-agent dispatch policies are exposed to the planner on the next turn; the traces also feed back into task construction. The three agents share the same underlying policy; the loop closes because the log of what the deployed agent failed at rewrites both the training corpus and the runtime retrieval.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Compared to open-source mobile-agent recipes (AndroidWorld / Mobile-Agent-v3 line and the AppWorld-style stateful function-calling line), the loop's closure is what buys the paper its cost efficiency: the +9.83 Overall gain over its own base model comes without a new base pretrain and without human-written new tasks — the tasks are generated, the reward is auto-calibrated by CARE, and the harness is auto-tuned by the Scenario Adapter. The paper's positioning against prior agentic RL pipelines (Agent-RLVR, Kimi-K3-K2 recipe, Rubric-guided RL) is that those recipes fix one leak at a time; this pipeline closes all three under the same policy.
  • Named contribution — CARE: Competence-Aware Reward-and-Advantage Engineering.
    • Problem. Fixed reward shaping on agentic tasks fails in both directions. If the reward is generous (partial credit for any correct tool call), the model learns to over-plan, over-invoke tools, and produce long trajectories whose extra tokens do not translate into task-outcome gains. If the reward is strict (all-or-nothing verifier), the RL never sees a gradient signal at the current competence and the model collapses toward short, degenerate policies. A hybrid-environment setup exacerbates the problem: the same policy is trained across executable-tool tasks, memory-recall tasks, skill-invocation tasks and sub-agent dispatch tasks, each with a different natural verifier scale.
    • Mechanism. CARE replaces a single fixed reward by two coupled adjustments to standard PPO-style RL. First, competence-aware reward: for each hybrid environment the reward function is scaled by an estimated competence score for that environment, so that partial credit lands where the current policy is weakest and disappears where it is already at ceiling — this keeps every environment producing gradient. Second, competence-aware advantage: within a group of sampled trajectories on the same task, the advantage baseline (the group's mean return) is corrected by the same competence estimator, so that easy environments do not swamp the batch and hard ones do not vanish. The competence estimate is refreshed online during training. The result: SFT lays down the skills and hybrid-environment RL under CARE raises reliability on tasks the base model already got right sometimes, and improves recovery on tasks it got wrong.
    • Why. This is a scale-and-shift on top of the standard PPO / GRPO family, not a new optimiser. Compared to plain GRPO (Kimi K3, DeepSeek-V4), CARE's per-environment scaling is what lets one policy be trained end-to-end across four qualitatively different environments (tool / memory / skill / sub-agent) without either the easy environment or the hard one dominating.
  • Named contribution — The Harness (Memory + Skills + Scenario Adapter + sub-agent dispatch).
    • Problem. A capable planner still fails on a mobile task if its runtime scaffolding is wrong. Memories that were retrieved based on prompt similarity may miss the entity the user actually meant (implicit-reference tasks). A skill retrieved from a static catalogue may be invoked at the wrong argument scale. A sub-agent dispatch policy that always fires the strongest sub-agent burns budget on trivial subtasks. And unlike the training loop, the deployed harness has to make these calls one turn at a time.
    • Mechanism. The Harness is a stateful execution component with four cooperating sub-modules. (i) Memory retrieves relevant evidence from histories that exceed the active context window, and is tested externally on LoCoMo, LongMemEval and BEAM; retrieval is evidence-grounded rather than similarity-only. (ii) Skills are tool-conditioned reusable procedural components (a lightweight, deployable representation of the trained policy's typical multi-tool routines) and are invoked when a matching pattern is detected in the plan. (iii) The Scenario Adapter matches runtime guidance to available tools and to the user's declared scenario, so that the same underlying skill is invoked with the correct argument scale (e.g. currency, date, permission scope) even when the user's prompt omits it. (iv) Sub-agent dispatch decomposes a request and delegates specialised subtasks (search / calculation / long-form drafting) to specialised sub-agents; the planner tracks constraints and dispatch history across turns and coordinates recovery when a tool call errors.
    • Why. This is the same harness class used by Kimi-K3's agent stack and the GPT-6 Astra-family Skills mechanism, but with two closed-loop distinctives: the Scenario Adapter's guidance library is populated from the deployed trace log, and Skills are compiled from RL-trained routines rather than authored by hand.

WanPE: Towards Cinematic Prompt Enhancement for Modern Text-to-Video Generation

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-25 Text-to-video · Prompt enhancement · RL post-training · Cinematic planning

Overview

  • Yubo Zhu, Yawen Shao, Ziyun Dai, Zixun Fang, Kai Zhu, Siyang Sun, Haolan Xue, Chuxin Wang, Tingyu Weng, Jingming Luo, Chen Shi, Lianghua Huang, Yufeng Ai, Yuzheng Wang, Wenyuan Zhang, Yu Shang, Yuxiang Bao, Zoubin Bi, Jie Xiao, Jinbo Xing, Jiaxing Zhao, Chongyang Zhong and additional co-authors — Wan Team, Alibaba Group, in collaboration with Nanjing University, USTC, Fudan and Tsinghua. arXiv:2609.30221v1 [cs.CV], submitted 2026-09-25, on the Hugging Face Daily selection for 2026-09-25. The Alibaba Wan Team affiliation is stated on the paper's project page (wan-pe.github.io) and puts the paper on the same Alibaba video-generation line as the previously-covered Wansong v1.0 (2026-07-17), Sana Video 2.0 and Wan3.0 public beta (2026-08-10).
  • The problem is that a raw user prompt fed to a modern text-to-video generator under-specifies the shot: it names an object and an action but not the camera trajectory, the lens, the lighting, the pacing, the cut, or the shot-level continuity across a 30-second clip. Human-written cinematic screenplays specify all of those. Existing prompt enhancers (Sora-style prompt rewriters, CAPE-T2V's captioner-anchored enhancement) either lose the user's specific semantic requirements while adding cinematic detail, or add cinematic detail at short durations but drift on longer ones as the shot list has to be sustained across cuts.
  • Contribution: a video-grounded reverse-construction training objective (write the shot-level cinematic plan from the finished video, not from the raw prompt), a 397B-parameter enhancer trained on 1.05M real-world videos, and a semantic-consistency RL algorithm (SC-GRPO) that keeps the user's declared content intact under the elaboration. Evaluated on the paper's own WanPEval benchmark (~11,000 human preference judgments across seven cinematic genres), WanPE adds 10.7–18.8 preference points over the raw prompt at 5–15-second clips and 50.9 points at 30 seconds, and remains competitive with Seedance 2.5 at 30 seconds while beating other commercial prompt enhancers at 5–15-second lengths.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Video-grounded reverse construction.
    • Problem. Training a prompt enhancer with (short-prompt → long-prompt) supervision has no ground truth: any given short prompt has many possible cinematic elaborations, and the elaboration a human labeller writes is not causally connected to a real video. When such a model is deployed against a finished generator, it often adds cinematic detail that the generator cannot actually render, so preference against the raw prompt is lost.
    • Mechanism. The pipeline reverses the causal order. (1) Start from 1.05M real-world videos with cinematic structure. (2) For each video, extract the shot list, camera trajectory, lens, lighting cues and pacing to produce a screenplay-grade long prompt L that a strong human director would write if commissioned to produce that exact video. (3) Reduce L to a short user-style prompt S by removing the cinematography. (4) Train the enhancer on (S → L) pairs. Because L is derived from a real, generator-renderable video, the enhancer's targets are the elaborations that a modern text-to-video generator can actually execute.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Compared to Sora-style captioner-first prompt rewriting (the enhancer is a captioner that runs on user prompts) and to CAPE-T2V's captioner-anchored enhancement (which conditions on both sides but constructs L from human labellers), reverse construction ties the target elaboration to the generator's rendering distribution. The 1.05M-video training corpus is what covers the seven genres reported on the project page (Action, Animation, Speech, Advertisement, Singing & Dance, Drama, Knowledge), each with its own cinematography conventions.
  • Named contribution — SC-GRPO (Semantic-Consistency GRPO).
    • Problem. Once the enhancer is elaborating aggressively, it can hallucinate content that was not in the user's short prompt. In a 30-second output that ripples into visible drift: the subject the user named in the prompt is replaced by a similar-but-different subject in later shots. Preference scores go up because the video is cinematically stronger, but the user's request has been silently rewritten.
    • Mechanism. SC-GRPO replaces the plain preference reward with a compound reward whose second term is a semantic-consistency check between the user's short prompt S, the generated elaboration L', and (in an on-policy loop) the video the generator produces from L'. GRPO's group-baseline structure is retained; the semantic-consistency term is subtracted from any elaboration that drops or replaces user-specified entities, and added to any elaboration that preserves them while extending cinematography. Because it operates on GRPO's advantage baseline rather than on the reward alone, easy prompts do not swamp hard ones.
    • Why. This is directly comparable to the Rubric-Guided Policy Optimization (RGPO) line for video reward modelling (RewardVerse, 2609.22947) — both split a preference reward into interpretable sub-terms. SC-GRPO's specific split (cinematic preference vs semantic consistency) is what buys the paper the 30-second gain: at short durations semantic drift is not visible, but at 30 seconds the consistency term is what stops the enhancer from silently rewriting the user's request.

Evaluation & Results

  • Primary benchmark: WanPEval, the paper's own preference-scored evaluation with ~11,000 human judgments across seven cinematic genres (Action, Animation, Speech, Advertisement, Singing & Dance, Drama, Knowledge). Preference score is delta over the raw user prompt on the same underlying generator.
  • Duration curve: at 5-, 10-, and 15-second clips WanPE adds 10.7–18.8 preference points over the raw prompt. At 30-second clips the gain grows to 50.9 points — the durations at which raw user prompts most under-specify the shot are also the durations where cinematic-plan enhancement helps most.
  • Competitor set (from the paper's project page): WanPE beats other commercial prompt enhancers at 5–15-second clip lengths and remains competitive with Seedance 2.5 at 30-second clips. The exact per-competitor preference deltas are in the paper's WanPEval table; the project page reports only the aggregate.
  • Scale: 397B parameters. This is the largest prompt-enhancement model reported to date; prior enhancer lines (CAPE-T2V, Sora-style rewriter) operate at sub-10B scale. The paper's positioning is that cinematic elaboration is closer to open-ended screenplay writing than to captioning, and the 397B parameter budget is what buys the seven-genre coverage.

Ablations

  • Reverse construction vs forward captioning: the paper's Section 5 ablation contrasts the video-grounded reverse-construction supervision (targets are elaborations derived from real videos) against a forward-captioning baseline (elaborations written by human labellers with no video). The reverse-construction training corpus produces elaborations that the downstream generator can actually render; the forward-captioning training corpus produces elaborations that trigger generator failure modes on longer clips.
  • SC-GRPO on / off: the semantic-consistency term is what closes the 30-second gap; without it, the paper reports the enhancer at 30 seconds drops several preference points because entities the user named in S are silently replaced in later shots. The exact drop is in the paper's ablation table.

Availability

  • Project page and evaluation artefacts: wan-pe.github.io. GitHub repository is referenced from the project page. The enhancer weights are not directly linked from the arXiv abstract or the project page.

ExplorationBench: Measuring AI Systems' Exploration in Verifiable Alien Worlds

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-24 Benchmark · Exploration · Rule discovery · Verifiable evaluation

Overview

  • Ming Zhang, Zhenghao Xiang, Peizhong Gao, Yujiong Shen, Yuhui Wang, Zhonghan Yue, Shihan Dou, Zhangyue Yin, Junjie Ye, Shichun Liu, Weihuang Zheng, Jiahao Chen, Jiayi Chen, Hongzhang Liu, Jiaqi Shao, Tao Gui, Qi Zhang, Xuanjing Huang, Suncong Zheng and Maxm Pan — Tencent Hunyuan (Suncong Zheng, Maxm Pan) with Fudan University NLP Group (Ming Zhang, Tao Gui, Qi Zhang, Xuanjing Huang and coauthors). arXiv:2609.30199v1 [cs.LG/cs.AI], submitted 2026-09-24, on the Hugging Face Daily selection for 2026-09-25. Tencent Hunyuan's coauthorship qualifies the paper under the frontier-labs filter.
  • The problem is that current agentic-reasoning benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, HLE, LiveCodeBench, SWE-Bench) grade recall over training-data-adjacent facts and procedures. When a frontier model scores well, that says almost nothing about its ability to explore — to sit in an environment whose rules it has never seen, form hypotheses, design experiments and iterate to a working rule. The confounds are two: an LLM judge can't verify a novel-rule hypothesis exactly, and if the rule matches something the model has seen in training, recall wins and exploration is never used.
  • Contribution: two verifiable alien-world sandboxes, both graded by exact executable checks. AlienCode is an alien calculation language with 31 discovery targets (hidden operators / constructs) and 70 held-out tasks that require applying those rules; grading is an executable interpreter over the language. AlienLogic is a patched deduction system with 24 discovery targets and 70 held-out tasks; grading is an executable proof checker. Each sandbox gives the system a flawed manual, worked examples, and a dedicated tool-call schema to run experiments. The paper evaluates 10 AI systems including Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, and top open-weight peers, and reports that even the best explorers are unstable across trajectories.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Verifiable alien worlds.
    • Problem. A rule-discovery benchmark faces two adversaries: (i) an LLM judge cannot exactly verify whether a hypothesised rule matches the hidden rule the world enforces — two rules can be semantically similar and produce different answers on the held-out tasks; and (ii) if the hidden rule is anywhere in the model's training distribution, recall obscures exploration. So the target has to be both executable (checkable exactly) and alien (conflicting with familiar knowledge).
    • Mechanism. Each sandbox is a deterministic executable system. AlienCode is a calculation language where the operators and control flow are defined in the sandbox's interpreter (not documented in the manual); AlienLogic is a deduction system whose axioms are patched so that familiar first-order-logic shortcuts fail. Both come with a flawed user manual: a document that overlaps in vocabulary with the true rule set but is missing or contradictory in the specifics the discovery targets test. The system is granted four exploration rounds (M1–M4) during which it may call the tool schema to run inputs through the interpreter / proof checker and observe outputs; on the held-out tasks the same interpreter / proof checker grades the system's answers.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The exact-grading property is what makes ExplorationBench a legitimate exploration test rather than a novel-flavoured reading-comprehension test. Compared to prior verifiable-reasoning benchmarks (NewtonBench-style scientific-law discovery, or the more established SWE-Bench-style code-verifier benchmarks), the alien-worlds design specifically breaks training-data recall: the interpreter's semantics are constructed to conflict with familiar programming or logical intuitions, so a model that has memorised a similar-looking language cannot silently pass by pattern-matching.
  • Named contribution — Autonomous exploration vs hindsight-replay evaluation.
    • Problem. A benchmark that only reports a system's final held-out score conflates two things: what the system discovered and how good its experimental protocol was at extracting that discovery. Two systems can end at the same score for different reasons: one designed better experiments, one was luckier with the environment's evidence sequence.
    • Mechanism. The paper distinguishes two evaluation modes on the same sandbox. In autonomous exploration, the system chooses which inputs to run through the tool schema at each round. In hindsight replay, the system is given the same sequence of (input, output) pairs an autonomous run produced, but not the freedom to choose them. Comparing scores between modes attributes gains to adaptive experiment design.
    • Why. On AlienCode, median autonomous-exploration score is 66.0% and median hindsight-replay is 40.7% — a 25.3-point gap that specifically measures the value of choosing which experiments to run. Prior benchmarks that only measure the final score cannot separate these components, so a system that got lucky with an easy evidence sequence looks the same as a system that designed better experiments.
  • Named contribution — Rule-report / task-solve gap.
    • Problem. A system that can state the correct rule at the end of the exploration phase might still not solve tasks that use it, because rule articulation and rule-execution are separate capabilities in current LLMs.
    • Mechanism. The paper additionally asks the system to report the discovered rule at the end of exploration, and grades the reported rule against the ground-truth rule (also via the interpreter / proof checker) independently of the held-out task score.
    • Why. Systems whose reported rule matches the ground-truth rule still solve the held-out tasks only 70.9% of the time. The rule report is a lossy readout of what the system can do: articulation and execution do not coincide.

  • Autonomous exploration vs hindsight replay on AlienCode: median 66.0% autonomous vs 40.7% hindsight replay — a 25.3-point gap that isolates the contribution of adaptive experiment design. Systems that plan their experiments materially outperform systems that see the same evidence in a fixed order.
  • Run-to-run variance reaches 72.8 points across trajectories on the same sandbox for the same system. Exploration is not monotonic: 6 of 30 AlienCode trajectories tracked finished below earlier milestones the same trajectory had reached, i.e. later rounds actively unlearned rules the earlier rounds had discovered.
  • Rule-report / task-solve gap: systems whose end-of-exploration rule report exactly matches the ground-truth rule still solve held-out tasks only 70.9% of the time. The paper reads this as a discovery-execution gap: articulating a rule and reliably executing under it are separate capabilities.
  • Sandbox-to-sandbox transfer: the paper reports transfer tests where a system that has explored one sandbox is tested on held-out tasks in the other. Transfer is not directly reported on the project page; the paper's Section 5 gives the per-sandbox transfer scores.

Ablations

  • Flawed manual on / off: removing the flawed manual (leaving only the tool schema) hurts exploration for the smaller systems in the panel and is roughly a wash for the top explorers — the manual is a scaffolding for weaker models and near-zero information for models that can compensate by running experiments.
  • Exploration round budget: performance is measured at M1, M2, M3, and M4. The paper's headline finding — that continued exploration can stall or reverse gains — is directly visible in the round-by-round trace, where the mean score across systems does not monotonically increase from M1 to M4. For some systems, M4 is worse than M3 because later hypotheses overwrite earlier correct beliefs.
  • Grader choice: the paper contrasts its executable-interpreter / proof-checker grading with LLM-judge grading on the same held-out task set. LLM-judge grading over-credits semantically-similar-but-wrong rule reports; exact executable grading rejects them.

Safety & Limitations

  • The two sandboxes are engineered against training-data recall, but the paper acknowledges that a system's meta-strategies (experiment design, hypothesis revision, belief tracking) do transfer from training data — the benchmark tests exploration skill, not exploration from scratch.
  • Two sandboxes are not a broad enough sample of alien-rule structures to claim generality; the paper positions ExplorationBench as a first, verifiable, executable-graded probe rather than a comprehensive exploration evaluation. Extending to more sandboxes with different rule families is flagged as future work.

Availability

  • Project page: www.explorationbench.com. arXiv paper: 2609.30199. Both sandboxes are released as open evaluation artefacts with their interpreters and proof checkers.
