Named Contributions & Mechanisms

  • Single-model listen-reason-tool-speak loop (no cascade).
    • Problem. Cascaded voice stacks (ASR → text LLM with tool-use → TTS) accumulate ASR errors, lose paralinguistic cues (prosody, overlap, backchanneling), and add fixed per-stage latency that cannot be hidden behind streaming because each stage waits on the one upstream. The resulting system cannot start speaking until the text LLM finishes, which is the dominant source of >1 s time-to-first-audio in prior art.
    • Mechanism. One model consumes raw speech, runs reasoning (with the reasoning.effort dial as a budget knob analogous to extended-thinking modes on text LLMs), calls tools (file_search, web_search, x_search, mcp, function) directly in the voice loop, and emits speech. The turn_detection and resumption session fields expose the full-duplex scheduler to the integrator, so an interruption flips the model from speaking to listening without a round-trip through a separate controller.
    • Why it works. Collapsing the pipeline into one model is what makes the Full Duplex Bench 97.0% result possible: interruptions, pauses, and backchannels are modeled at the token level alongside content, not as a separate orchestration layer. It also explains the task-success margin on blind calls (94.8% vs 87.4% for GPT-Live-1) — the model preserves acoustic context across tool calls, so re-grounding after a long web_search does not require re-listening.
  • Price-flat upgrade with cost-per-task compression.
    • Problem. A voice-agent price change invalidates every production SLA and routing table that depends on it, and most voice-stack upgrades (Grok Voice 2.0 → Grok Voice 3.0) ship with a price bump that forces integrators to redo cost modeling. Enterprises deploying at Tesla or Starlink scale cannot absorb that renegotiation per upgrade.
    • Mechanism. xAI holds minute-pricing flat at $0.08/min and auto-migrates the grok-voice-latest alias. Cost-per-task compression comes from Big Bench Audio accuracy gains at identical minute pricing: at 98.5% accuracy, more calls terminate successfully per billable minute than at Grok Voice 2.0's 97.2%. Artificial Analysis's dollars-per-task figure ($0.025) captures this.
    • Why it works. A flat-price upgrade means every production deployment picks up the gain without touching contracts. The GPT-Realtime-2.1 (High) comparison at $0.055 per Big Bench Audio task (over 2× more expensive) is specifically pitched at Tesla/Starlink-scale call-center integrators, who are the stated beneficiaries in the launch post.
  • MCP as a first-class voice-agent tool.
    • Problem. Voice-agent integrations usually bolt backend connectivity on through generic function-call handlers, which forces every integrator to re-implement authentication, schema negotiation, and streaming for every backend they want to reach. MCP already solves this for text agents but was absent from most Realtime APIs.
    • Mechanism. mcp appears as a session-level tool alongside function, file_search, web_search, and x_search. A voice session configured with mcp reaches any MCP-exposed backend without a per-backend function shim, and the Tesla "Connectors" surface (calendar, email, order flows) and Starlink's single-agent flow (verification, troubleshooting, credits, replacement shipment) run on top of this.
    • Why it works. First-class MCP support means the voice agent inherits any new MCP-exposed capability without a redeploy. The Starlink metric (one agent handling verification → troubleshooting → credit → replacement in ~20,000 daily calls, ~70% resolved without human handoff) is only tractable if the agent can touch multiple backends inside one session, which is what mcp gives it.

HICO-Bench & PaCPO: Verifiable Social Interactions in Paired Worlds

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv · Tongyi-ConvAI (Alibaba) 2026-10-14 Social agents · Benchmarks · RL · Verifiable rewards · Paired-worlds

Overview

  • Alibaba Tongyi-ConvAI (Caixin Kang, Ke Wang, Yuchuan Wu, Xiaoqiang Wang, Ling Li, Bingxue Qiu, Yongbin Li, Yoichi Sato) argues that social-agent evaluation has over-indexed on LLM-judge "goal-completion" scores applied to the conversation surface and under-tested whether the plan the agents actually arrive at would work.
  • The paper has two artifacts: HICO-Bench, a paired-worlds benchmark that checks both the feasibility of the agreed plan against each agent's private conditions and the integrity of the conversation that produced it; and PaCPO (Paired-Credit Policy Optimization), an RL algorithm that assigns dense per-turn credit by probing tentative plans against the paired worlds.
  • Headline framing: across 13 LLMs, 36% of conversations that score highly on goal completion still fail Verified Success. The split-level finding is the paper's contribution to the fluent-but-wrong failure mode in social agents.

Methodology

  • HICO-Bench construction. 180 everyday coordination scenarios across three categories (resource allocation, scheduling, authorization). Each scenario splits into two agents each holding private information plus a shared public context. The two agents converse, then independently record their understanding of the agreed plan.
  • Dual check.
    • Feasibility (programmatic). The two independently recorded plans must match each other and satisfy both agents' private conditions. Checked by executable program against the scenario's constraints, not by an LLM judge.
    • Integrity (LLM judge). The conversation must support the recorded plan — the agents cannot have landed on a correct plan without discussing it, or landed on an incorrect plan while sounding like they discussed a correct one.
    • Verified Success. Both checks must pass. This is the headline metric that 36% of highly rated conversations fail.
  • Paired worlds. Each scenario has two versions with identical public context but one differing private condition, so the correct plan differs between worlds. The paired construction is what lets PaCPO assign tentative-plan credit at turn level: a tentative plan after turn t can be checked against both versions, and only a plan that fits its version earns reward.
  • PaCPO (Paired-Credit Policy Optimization).
    • Problem. Standard social-agent RL rewards only arrive at conversation end, so the policy update attributes gain to the full trajectory without separating turns that locked in the correct plan from turns that padded.
    • Mechanism. After each turn, the agent is prompted for a tentative plan. The tentative plan is checked against both paired-world versions of the scenario. Turns whose tentative plan fits the agent's own version are rewarded; turns whose tentative plan matches the other version are not. Dense per-turn signal is then rolled up into the policy update.
    • Why it works. The paired-worlds construction gives the reward a counterfactual anchor that a single-world checker cannot: a plan that happens to work in both versions is uninformative, but a plan that distinguishes correctly between versions is clear evidence the agent used its private information. This matches the paper's framing that effective social interaction is more than fluent output: the signal rewards actually-used private information, not fluency.

Evaluation & Results

  • Headline cross-model result. Across 13 LLMs evaluated on HICO-Bench, 36% of conversations rated highly on goal completion fail Verified Success. The gap is the paper's measurement that goal-completion scores are a weak proxy.
  • PaCPO on Qwen3-8B. Verified Success 20.4% → 28.3% (+7.9 pp absolute).
  • Per-model success-rate breakdowns, per-category (resource allocation / scheduling / authorization) splits, and per-check (feasibility vs integrity) ablations are reported in the paper but not in the abstract.

Other

  • HICO-Bench is positioned as a drop-in replacement for conversational-social-agent evaluations that score only on fluency and goal-completion; the paired-worlds trick is reusable for any social task where private information is central to the correct plan.

Cortex: Consolidating Context into Parametric Memory

Tier 2 · Technical Report arXiv · ByteDance Seed 2026-10-14 Context compression · LoRA · FFN edits · Sequential updates · Pareto

Overview

  • ByteDance Seed (Jintian Zhang, Feng He, Xinnian Liang, Ziwen Xu, Haoming Xu, Haojing Huang, Shuangzhi Wu, Ningyu Zhang) frames Memory Consolidation as a formal objective: retain the gain a model gets from a specific context after the context is removed, without disturbing the model's other abilities.
  • The paper opens with a five-task-family testbed covering single, batch, and sequential context updates. The contribution is Cortex, a method that generates prospective queries to anticipate how a context will be used, then distills context-conditioned behavior into mergeable low-rank or feed-forward-network updates.
  • Reported to sit on the consolidation / retention Pareto frontier in most evaluated settings across 4B-120B dense and MoE backbones, text-only and multimodal.
  • Key number: after 50 sequential factual edits on a 14B backbone, Cortex-Structural recovers 88.9% of the context-induced gain while retaining 99.8% of the original (non-target) performance — the kind of two-objective result that is normally traded off against each other in model-editing benchmarks.

Methodology

  • Problem framing. A context C gives a frozen model f a measurable gain on a target task family. The question is whether that gain can be baked into mergeable parameter deltas (LoRA or FFN edits) applied to f such that: (a) the gain holds without C at inference; (b) the model's other abilities are untouched; (c) multiple consolidations stack without catastrophic interference.
  • Prospective-query generation. Cortex synthesizes queries that anticipate how C would be used downstream — not a reconstruction of C's surface tokens, but a distribution over the usage patterns. This is what the method distills against, so the compressed deltas target the uses of C, not C verbatim.
  • Distillation into mergeable updates. Context-conditioned outputs from f+C on the prospective queries are the supervision signal; the student is a LoRA (Cortex-LoRA) or an FFN-structural edit (Cortex-Structural) applied back to f. Weights of f stay frozen; the deltas are what accumulate across consolidations.
  • Mergeable by construction. Because consolidations are LoRA or FFN deltas, batch and sequential update settings compose by summation (LoRA) or in-place application (FFN), which is what makes the 50-edit sequential result tractable without catastrophic drift on non-target behavior.
  • Testbed. Five task families covering single, batch, and sequential updates. Dense and MoE backbones from 4B to 120B, both text-only and multimodal.

The retention metric is the gate: a method that recovers context gain but destroys non-target performance is not a consolidation, which is why the 99.8% non-target retention sits next to the 88.9% recovery in the headline.

Player-1: Learning to Play from Synthetic Experience

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv · ARC Lab, Tencent 2026-10-14 Game agents · Computer-use · Synthetic environments · PPO · Composite actions

Overview

  • Tencent ARC Lab (Mingyu Ouyang, Yiran Wang, Siyuan Hu, Xingyilang Yin, Huiqiang Sun, Guangzhi Wang, Junfu Pu, Cheng Cheng, Honghao Chen, Ying Shan, Mike Zheng Shou, Hwee Tou Ng) argues that game-agent pipelines bottleneck on demonstration data: curated gameplay trajectories cover a narrow slice of dynamic game states, and policies trained purely by imitation fail to generalize.
  • Player-1 is a four-part post-training recipe: an extended action space (relative pointer motion, sustained inputs, composite actions on top of the standard computer-use action space); supervised initialization on 6,044 curated gameplay trajectories pairing recorded actions with task instructions and visual reasoning; Player-1-Gym, 16 synthetic 2D/3D environments built around control demands shared across games; and PPO reinforcement learning with programmatically verifiable rewards, randomized observations, and randomized control dynamics.
  • Two released models: Player-1-9B and Player-1-35B-A3B (MoE, 3B active).
  • Headline result on MineDojo-300: Player-1-9B 38.33% vs 12.63% for the strongest evaluated baseline, with +27.8 pp over its own SFT policy.

Methodology

  • Extended action space. The default computer-use action space (discrete key/button presses, absolute pointer position) is too coarse for game control. Player-1 adds (i) relative pointer motion (velocity vector rather than target coordinates, so the model can specify "pan slightly right" without recomputing an absolute click target); (ii) sustained inputs (hold-key duration as a first-class parameter rather than a repeated-press stream); and (iii) composite actions (multiple keyboard and mouse inputs issued at the same timestep, so e.g. move-while-looking in a 3D environment is one action instead of two interleaved).
  • Supervised initialization. 6,044 curated gameplay trajectories; each sample pairs the recorded action with task instructions and visual reasoning (what the model sees, what the model is trying to do, which action it takes). The SFT policy is the warm-start for PPO rather than the final policy.
  • Player-1-Gym (synthetic environments). 16 2D and 3D synthetic environments explicitly designed around control demands that transfer across games — precise aim-and-shoot, 3D navigation under occlusion, timed button-press puzzles, and so on. Programmatically verifiable rewards: no reward model, no human judge, no LLM judge.
  • PPO with randomization. Rollouts across Player-1-Gym with randomized observations (visual perturbations, texture swaps) and randomized control dynamics (input lag, pointer sensitivity). The pairing of randomization dimensions is explicit domain randomization, with the stated goal of transfer to unseen games (MineDojo and ViZDoom were not in Player-1-Gym).

Named Contributions & Mechanisms

  • Composite actions as a reusable control primitive.
    • Problem. Standard computer-use models emit one input per step: keyboard press, mouse click, or scroll. For a game that requires simultaneous movement + aim + fire, one-input-per-step forces the policy to interleave actions across many timesteps, which blows up credit assignment and bounds reaction time by the model's inference latency.
    • Mechanism. Composite actions let a single policy step emit a set of keyboard and mouse inputs applied at the same simulation tick. The policy still reasons in text/visual space before emitting, so the composite is a learned skill rather than a hard-coded macro.
    • Why it works. The paper reports that composite-action usage rises during RL — the RL loop discovers that composite inputs are what unlocks the dense-reward tail of each environment. Removing composites from the action space collapses success rate on 3D-navigation and aim-and-shoot environments, which is why the extended action space precedes PPO in the recipe rather than following it.
  • Player-1-Gym as a transferable training surface.
    • Problem. Training a game-playing agent on a specific game produces a specialist that does not generalize to other games. Training on human gameplay demonstrations covers only the states a competent human visits, which is a narrow slice of possible game states, especially the failure-adjacent ones.
    • Mechanism. Sixteen synthetic environments designed around control-demand classes (precise aim, 3D navigation under occlusion, timed presses, etc.), not around specific games. Each is instrumented with a programmatic reward, so PPO reward signals are not polluted by an LLM judge. Randomized observations and control dynamics are applied per rollout.
    • Why it works. Transfer from the synthetic set to real games is the headline evidence: Player-1-9B improves +27.8 pp over SFT on MineDojo-300 despite Minecraft not being in Player-1-Gym; Player-1-35B-A3B improves +17.7 pp. The ablation that broader synthetic coverage improves transfer is the paper's key finding on why generic skill primitives beat game-specific demonstrations at this scale.
