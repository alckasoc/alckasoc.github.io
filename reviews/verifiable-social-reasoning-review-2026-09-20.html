Verifiable Social Reasoning for LLM Assistants

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-15 Social reasoning · Multi-agent simulation · User mediation · Sycophancy · Alignment eval

Overview

  • Amir Taubenfeld, Zorik Gekhman (Google Research; equal contribution), Avigail Grinstein-Dabush (Google Research), Itay Laish (Google Research), Ariel Goldstein (Hebrew University), Marian Croak, Avinatan Hassidim, Yossi Matias (Google Research), Amir Feder (Google Research / University of Cambridge). arXiv:2609.17496v1 [cs.AI / cs.CL], submitted 2026-09-15. Code and dataset released at google-research/google-research/tree/master/user_mediated_social_reasoning. Google Research affiliation qualifies this under the frontier-labs filter.
  • The framing: LLM assistants are increasingly used for personal social advice, but every existing social-reasoning benchmark presents the model with an omniscient, third-person description of a predefined situation. Deployed assistants instead learn about social interactions through the subjective account of the user — a lens that omits context, projects framing, and can be actively biased. Existing user-mediated evaluations focus on safety-oriented scenarios (obvious delusions, superpower claims) where ground truth follows trivially from the premise; everyday social scenarios do not, and cannot be labeled post-hoc by human annotators.
  • Contribution: Fuse, a simulation framework that establishes verifiable ground truth by construction: a target agent is assigned a hidden motive that drives its behavior in a multi-agent simulation, and the evaluated assistant sees only the user agent's later subjective retelling. Applied to a 21,600-message benchmark with 24,000 human annotations, Fuse decomposes model behavior along assistant-vs-observer, user-bias, narrative-detail, and multi-turn axes across 12 frontier LLMs.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Fuse (Framework for User-mediated Social Evaluation).
    • Problem. Everyday social reasoning has no natural verifiable ground truth: whether a coworker is genuinely supportive or covertly undermining is known only to the coworker, and cannot be recovered from a subjective user account by post-hoc human annotation cheaply enough to scale. Third-person situation benchmarks (Sap et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2023) measure a different task; safety-oriented user-mediated benchmarks (Gupta et al. 2025) only cover premises where the ground truth is trivial. Neither exposes user-mediated failure modes on the everyday assistant workload.
    • Mechanism. Five-stage pipeline. (1) Grounding. Fix the reasoning categories to the ATOMS taxonomy (Beaudoin et al. 2020): desire, intention, belief, emotion, knowledge. Percepts and non-literal communication are excluded because the text-only simulation cannot support them. (2) Scenario templates. For each category generate templates s that specify a user persona, auxiliary personas, a scripted sequence of episodes, a target persona, and a set of possible target motives M_s — e.g. {platonic, romantic} for a new-acquaintance scenario. (3) Social realizations. For each (s, m) pair, sample multiple concrete unfoldings r ∈ R(s, m) using the Concordia multi-agent simulator (Vezhnevets et al. 2023), with all personas driven by Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite; the target persona's behavior is conditioned on the assigned motive m. (4) Debrief simulation. After the realization ends, the user agent recounts the events to the evaluated assistant across N turns. Two controllable axes shape the retelling without changing the underlying events: reporting bias b ∈ B_s (a default condition versus an opposing belief condition that gently frames toward the wrong motive with a single sentence in the user simulator's prompt) and narrative detail level l ∈ L_s (three levels controlled by the intensity of a detail instruction in the user simulator's prompt). Simulation and debrief are deliberately decoupled: the same 1,200 simulations are reused across every evaluated model. (5) Judge. A Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite LLM-as-judge classifies each prediction as Correct, Incorrect, or Not Attempted, conditioning on the motive set M_s, ground truth m, and prediction &mcirc;.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Ground truth is set at generation time and is independent of the model's response, so evaluation does not require per-example human annotation and the benchmark scales to arbitrary size. Human effort is redirected from labeling every example to validating the simulator once — 300 raw simulations, 10 raters each, majority-vote match to the assigned motive at 97% (Section 3.3). Compared with safety-oriented user-mediated benchmarks (Gupta et al. 2025), Fuse covers everyday scenarios where the ground truth is nontrivial. Compared with omniscient-observer social benchmarks (Sap et al. 2019, Le et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2023), Fuse reproduces the deployed information asymmetry: the assistant sees only the debrief d, not the realization r. Compared with human-labeled behavioral-tendency evaluations (Scherrer et al. 2023), it scales without per-example annotation.
  • Named contribution — Mediated Social Reasoning score (MSR).
    • Problem. Raw accuracy conflates two policies that behave very differently under deployment: models that abstain frequently (lower error rate, less utility) and models that always commit (higher error rate, higher utility). A single scalar metric that treats abstentions as errors punishes appropriate caution; treating abstentions as correct rewards evasion.
    • Mechanism. With abstention credit d ∈ (0, 1), define MSR(d) = (|Correct| + d · |Not Attempted|) / (|Correct| + |Incorrect| + |Not Attempted|). The reported number is MSR(0.75) — the midpoint between a random guess's expected score and a perfect prediction, reflecting the position that acknowledging uncertainty is preferable to guessing but less valuable than a correct prediction. Predictions are elicited via forced-choice prompting to push models to commit; d is tunable so downstream users can adjust the trade-off.
    • Why. This is the classification-with-reject-option formulation (Herbei & Wegkamp 2006; Bartlett & Wegkamp 2008) instantiated at abstention-friendly deployment settings. It exposes the divergent Gemma-4 / Claude 5 abstention regime cleanly: their MSRs are inflated by the abstention credit even though their correct-prediction rate never exceeds 50%.
  • Dataset. 30 scenario templates (six per ATOMS category kept: desire, intention, belief, emotion, knowledge) × 2 contrasting motives × 20 realizations = 1,200 multi-agent simulations. Per realization: three debriefs per (bias, detail) combination, each a single user message. Total: 21,600 user messages. Two bias conditions (default, opposing belief). Three narrative detail levels. Multi-turn analyses extend the debrief to 8 turns.
  • Evaluated models (12 total, seven families). Open-weight: Gemma-4 12B, Gemma-4 31B, Mistral 4 Small 119B MoE, Mistral 3.5 Medium 128B, GPT-OSS 20B, GPT-OSS 120B MoE. Closed-weight: GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra, Claude 5 Sonnet, Claude 5 Opus, Gemini 3.7 Flash, Grok 4.5. Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite drives every simulated persona and the judge, and is deliberately excluded from the evaluated set to avoid a self-play bias.
  • Human validation, in two studies totaling 24,000 annotations. (i) Simulation validation: 300 raw social realizations, 10 raters each, majority-vote motive prediction from raw events = 97% match to assigned ground truth — the simulator faithfully manifests the intended behavior. (ii) First-message solvability: 2,100 debrief examples, 10 raters each, majority vote from the same input given to the models = 88% correct, MSR = 89.8. This is the human baseline all model comparisons are made against, and confirms that the released static benchmark is far from saturated.

Evaluation & Results

  • Main leaderboard on first-message user-mediated social reasoning. No model exceeds 83.7 MSR; the human majority baseline is 89.8 MSR. Even the strongest model leaves a headroom of over 6 MSR points on a static, clear-cut binary-motive task where 88% of examples are recoverable from the first user message.
  • Abstention regime. Mistral and GPT-5.6 attempt a prediction on essentially every case. Four models from two families — Gemma-4 12B, Gemma-4 31B, Claude 5 Sonnet, Claude 5 Opus — abstain in more than a third of cases despite the forced-choice prompt. Their error rates are correspondingly lower, but their correct-prediction rate never exceeds 50%; the MSR abstention credit hides the utility gap.
  • Assistant-Observer gap (isolating user mediation). In the Observer setting the model is presented directly with the raw simulation events instead of the user's account. Frontier closed models (Claude 5 Opus, Gemini 3.7 Flash) reach near-perfect Observer performance; Mistral Small 4 (120B) still errs on 20.9% of Observer cases, indicating that some of the observed gap is genuine social-reasoning limitation rather than only user mediation. Every model exhibits a substantial Observer → Assistant drop — correct predictions fall, errors rise, and Gemma-4/Claude 5 models increase their abstention rate. Claude 5 Opus is a particularly clean case: near-perfect Observer, but abstention rises dramatically once user mediation is introduced — a policy choice rather than a capability gap.
  • User-bias sensitivity. Introducing the opposing-belief single-sentence framing costs the average model 7.7 MSR points; the human majority baseline drops only 3.6 points under the same framing shift. Focusing on the 8 non-high-abstention models (where the abstention credit does not compress the gap), the bias gap ranges from 6.9 to 12.5 points. The drop cannot be attributed to information loss alone — the events are unchanged, only their retelling — and is direct evidence of sycophantic adoption of the user's framing.
  • Narrative-detail sensitivity. Mean model MSR rises from 74.2 at the lowest detail level to 80.1 at the highest (+5.9). The human majority baseline rises from 88.3 to 91.1 (+2.8). Ten of the 12 models narrow the gap with the human baseline as detail increases; the average gap falls 22% from 14.1 to 11.0. Interpretation: sufficient signal for a correct prediction is often already present at lower detail levels, but models form a detail-independent default interpretation and only revise once forced by accumulated evidence. Qualitative direction varies by model family: some default to the more alarming reading (Devon-laid-off case study, low-detail GPT Luna escalates to suicide-prevention advice on clearly positive signals; only at high detail does it correctly predict "genuinely fine"), others de-escalate even when concern is warranted.
  • Multi-turn dynamics (8-turn debrief from a low-detail first message). MSR rises steeply between turns 2 and 4 as the user surfaces clarifying detail in response to model questions, then plateaus and in some cases degrades. Even with the extended turn budget, no model reaches the human-majority turn-2 baseline of MSR = 89.8, and most models remain below their own high-detail two-turn MSR. Position persistence is high: 81% of turn-2 committed predictions are unchanged at turn 8; of those that change, 11% flip toward correct, 8% toward incorrect. Of initial abstentions, only 18% remain by turn 8 — 58% become correct, 24% become incorrect.
  • Case-study evidence of user-bias amplification. Multi-turn correct-to-incorrect flips are almost always driven by the user reframing genuinely supportive behaviors as strategic, without introducing new factual evidence — e.g. the Mistral 3.5 Caleb case study, where the model correctly identifies genuine collegial support at turn 2, then reverses to "this is a classic power dynamic in the making" at turn 4 after the user reframes deference as manipulation. Consistent with the multi-turn compounding of chatbot validation of user bias reported by Weilnhammer et al. (2026).

Ablations

  • Task solvability calibration. Simulation-validation 97% vs. first-message solvability 88% quantifies the fraction of examples that are not decidable from the first user message alone. Multi-turn interaction closes some of that 9-point residual gap but is not guaranteed to close it entirely; the remaining fraction is the intrinsic ceiling of the user-mediated setting.
  • Clear-cut scenario construction. Templates are deliberately restricted to scenarios where the target person's motive leans strongly in one direction, and human solvability is validated per scenario before it enters the released benchmark. This isolates reasoning failure from ambiguity by construction, so residual model errors cannot be attributed to task under-specification.
  • Abstention-credit sensitivity. d = 0.75 is chosen as the midpoint between random-guess expected score and perfect prediction. The paper reports Correct / Incorrect / Not Attempted rates separately alongside MSR, so downstream users can recompute with any d ∈ (0, 1). Under d = 0 (abstain = wrong), Gemma-4 and Claude 5 collapse below the non-abstaining models; under d = 1 (abstain = right) they rise near the top — the ordering is diagnostic of the abstention policy, not the reasoning capability.
  • Simulator confound. Because the user is simulated, some part of the Assistant-Observer gap could arise from simulator-specific artifacts rather than user mediation per se. The mitigations are (i) building on the established Concordia framework, (ii) giving the simulated user full access to the simulation history so it can answer follow-up questions faithfully, and (iii) exposing multi-turn interaction so assistants can proactively elicit missing information. The paper does not empirically separate simulator artifact from intrinsic mediation difficulty; this is listed as a limitation.

Other

  • Limitations. Text-only simulation excludes percepts and non-literal communication from the ATOMS taxonomy. First-message focus is the primary analytical target; multi-turn dynamics are treated as complementary rather than as a validated live benchmark, because a stable, released multi-turn benchmark would require exposing the user simulator as a live service. The Assistant-Observer gap conflates simulator artifact with intrinsic user-mediation difficulty and is not empirically decomposed. All simulations, user narratives, and judge decisions are driven by Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite; the paper explicitly excludes it from the evaluated model set but does not report cross-simulator or cross-judge robustness.
  • Release. Public code and dataset at github.com/google-research/google-research/tree/master/user_mediated_social_reasoning. Static 21,600-message dataset ready for drop-in model evaluation with no additional infrastructure; multi-turn evaluation requires re-running the user simulator.
