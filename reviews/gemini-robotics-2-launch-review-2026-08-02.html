Gemini Robotics 2: Whole-Body Intelligence, Multi-Finger Dexterity, and Multi-Robot Collaboration

Tier 1 · Launch (blog + safety technical report + model cards) Google DeepMind blog 2026-07-30 Foundation robotics · Whole-body VLA · Embodied reasoning · Multi-robot orchestration · ASIMOV-Agentic safety

Overview

  • Author of the main launch blog: Carolina Parada (Sr. Director, Robotics). The launch groups three model releases and one companion technical report into a single Gemini Robotics 2 family:
    • Gemini Robotics 2 VLA — vision–language–action model that converts vision plus language input into motor control, capable of controlling full humanoids (Apptronik Apollo 2 with SharpaWave and Inspire hands) and bi-arm gripper platforms (Franka Duo with the Robotiq gripper) from a single checkpoint.
    • Gemini Robotics ER 2 — the most capable embodied reasoning (ER) model in the family. A vision–language model acting as the robot’s high-level brain: it understands the physical world, communicates with humans, plans multi-step tasks lasting several minutes, orchestrates the VLA and any user-declared tools, and can call Google Search or user-defined functions natively.
    • Gemini Robotics On-Device 2 — the most efficient VLA in the family, optimized to run locally on robotic devices with no network latency, and natively multi-embodiment.
    • Gemini Robotics 2: Safety Evaluations — companion technical report (2026-07-29, Gemini Robotics Team, Google DeepMind) introducing ASIMOV-Agentic, a new benchmark for agentic safety orchestration and uncertainty resolution; released as a Hugging Face dataset at google/asimov_agentic.
  • The launch positions Gemini Robotics 2 as a step change over Gemini Robotics 1.5 (September 2025) and Gemini Robotics ER 1.6 (April 2026) along four axes: (a) whole-body control (not just table-top upper-body), (b) multi-finger dexterity on 22-DoF hands and precise bi-arm gripper tasks, (c) agentic reasoning over continuous video with progress classification and moment finding, (d) multi-robot collaboration where heterogeneous robots (humanoid + fixed bi-arm) share a semantic representation and hand off subtasks.
  • Named contributions that receive mechanism explanations below: Single-checkpoint whole-body VLA, Motion-transfer On-Device VLA, Bidirectional-streaming ER-2 orchestration on the Gemini Live API, Continuous progress classification & moment finding, Multi-robot semantic collaboration, and the ASIMOV-Agentic safety benchmark.
  • Companion developer post: Introducing Gemini Robotics ER 2 (Steven Hansen, Peng Xu, 2026-07-30). Companion safety PDF: Gemini Robotics 2: Safety Evaluations.

Architecture

  • Three-model family with a clear division of labour: ER 2 as System 2 (agentic reasoning, planning, tool orchestration, safety) hands sub-tasks to VLA / On-Device VLA as System 1 (fast motor execution).
  • ER 2 uses a vision–language model backbone with a bidirectional-streaming endpoint on the Gemini Live API; developers declare low-level control interfaces (VLA models, navigation APIs) as tools, stream multimodal video / audio / text into the model, and the model calls those tools while continuing to reason in parallel — the launch calls out the resulting absence of “jarring stop-and-think pauses” between reasoning and action.
  • The VLA controls entire humanoids: it emits torque / joint targets that translate intent (natural-language instructions like “put the watering can into the green bin in the bottom shelf”) into a walking approach, an upper-body grasp, and a placement, staying in one policy across the whole trajectory rather than switching between locomotion and manipulation controllers.
  • Named contribution: single-checkpoint cross-embodiment whole-body VLA — problem, mechanism, why.
    • Problem. Previous Gemini Robotics VLAs controlled the upper body of a humanoid on table-top tasks only, and cross-embodiment transfer between qualitatively different bodies (five-finger dexterous hand vs. parallel-jaw gripper, humanoid legs vs. fixed base) required per-body fine-tuning. That gates deployment on both the diversity of hardware and the diversity of tasks a single model can handle.
    • Mechanism. The Gemini Robotics 2 VLA is trained to emit action tokens for a superset action space covering three concrete embodiments (Apollo 2 with SharpaWave 22-DoF hands, Apollo 2 with Inspire hands, Franka Duo with Robotiq grippers). At inference, the same model checkpoint is used across all three; the environment adapter maps the emitted actions to the target embodiment’s low-level command interface. On humanoids, the action space extends beyond arms and hands to include the base and torso, so a single forward pass can plan walking towards a table, crouching, grasping, and placing.
    • Why. The reported evaluation is done with a single checkpoint controlling all three embodiments, not per-body fine-tunes — the pick-from-floor (45.7%) task in particular is not achievable without whole-body coordination. This is the step change over Gemini Robotics 1.5, which controlled upper-body only.
  • Named contribution: Motion-Transfer On-Device 2 — problem, mechanism, why.
    • Problem. Robots operating in homes, warehouses, or industrial cells often need to run without network latency or connectivity, but on-device models historically lag frontier VLAs and adapt slowly to new hardware. New bi-arm robots with different sensor suites and degrees of freedom typically require thousands of teleoperated demonstrations.
    • Mechanism. On-Device 2 is a compressed, latency-optimized VLA that inherits the “motion transfer” technique introduced with Gemini Robotics 1.5. Motion transfer trains the model on a shared latent action representation across embodiments, so that adapting to a new bi-arm robot only needs to fit the small residual mapping from that latent representation to the new body’s command interface, rather than re-learning behaviors from scratch.
    • Why. The launch reports adaptation to entirely new bi-arm robot embodiments (Dexmate, SO101, Trossen platforms are the demonstrated examples) with typically <200 demonstration examples and adaptation completed in a few hours — the same-body performance carries over even when the new body has different shape, sensors, and DoF count.

Agentic reasoning & multi-robot orchestration

  • Named contribution: Continuous progress classification & moment finding — problem, mechanism, why.
    • Problem. Long-horizon robotic tasks (tightening a light bulb, tying a trash bag) fail silently: the VLA can execute a subtask but the orchestrator has no reliable signal to know when it is done, so it either transitions too early (dropping the bulb, leaving the bag untied) or too late (over-tightening, over-processing). Prior ER models scored progress from static snapshots and missed intra-episode failures.
    • Mechanism. ER 2 ingests continuous egocentric video and produces two grounded outputs per frame: (i) progress classification that assigns each frame to one of five progress buckets (0–20%, 20–40%, 40–60%, 60–80%, 80–100%) toward the current sub-task; (ii) moment finding that localizes the exact frame where a critical event happens (needle crosses a threshold, bag closes, cup fills). Both are used by the orchestrator to gate transitions, retry failed steps, or invoke safety tools; both run inside the same Live-API bidirectional stream so tool calls trigger without pausing perception.
    • Why. Reported numbers: progress classification at 57.4% accuracy, above prior-generation ER 1.6 and reported frontier competitors; moment finding at 91.3% accuracy with mean absolute distance 0.96s, at claimed 4× the execution speed and a fraction of the compute of larger frontier competitors. Sub-second moment-finding latency is what makes real-time safety stops physically feasible on a moving humanoid.
  • Named contribution: Multi-robot semantic collaboration — problem, mechanism, why.
    • Problem. A single robot morphology cannot cover all tasks (a wheeled rover navigates a warehouse floor well but has no arms; a humanoid can grasp but is slow indoors). Prior orchestrators handled one robot at a time.
    • Mechanism. ER 2 exposes a shared semantic representation across robots and a coordination protocol on top of the Live API. Robots communicate object-level state (“left tool on table A”, “arrived at shelf B”) rather than raw sensor streams, and the ER-2 orchestrator schedules subtasks across bodies — e.g., Apollo 2 hands off an object to Franka Duo, or the two collaborate on a bimanual task neither can complete alone.
    • Why. The launch demonstrates Apptronik’s Apollo 2 and Franka F3 Duo collaborating on a shared workflow. This is the first foundation-model robotics release from a major lab that treats multi-robot as a first-class capability rather than a special case — and it matters because homogeneous fleets are the exception, not the rule for real deployments.
  • The developer blog measures ER 2 tool orchestration performance in three modes: real-world VLA, simulation VLA, and human tele-op. ER 2 consistently outperforms ER 1.6 across all three modes (reported as a bar chart in the developer post).
  • Improvements in general spatial intelligence:
    • Success/failure detection now operates on raw video feeds rather than static snapshots, catching mid-execution failures like spills, slips, misalignments.
    • General instrument reading extends beyond circular dials and sight glasses to include digital displays, linear scales, rulers, and liquid thermometers — tested across 10 instrument types.
    • Enhanced spatial VQA on the ERQA benchmark.

Ablations

  • Progress classification bucket size. The safety report and developer post choose a five-bucket (0–20%, ..., 80–100%) discretization — enough granularity for the orchestrator to decide when to retry vs. advance, without forcing the model to solve arbitrary-precision progress regression.
  • Developer-instruction (DI) depth on VLA feasibility. The safety report ablates DI depth on the same ER 2 model: DI0 (no VLA-training-distribution summary) → 62.0% feasibility-classification accuracy; DI1, DI2 → intermediate; DI3 (most detailed training-instruction summary) → 95.8%. All frontier models benefit from feasibility awareness; the ceiling shifts up proportionally with in-context detail.
  • Instrument-reading generalization ablation. ER 2 is evaluated across 10 instrument categories (circular dials, sight glasses, digital displays, linear scales, rulers, liquid thermometers, ...) rather than only the circular-dial family that dominated ER 1.6 evaluations, and shows accuracy gains across all of them.
  • Real-VLA vs. sim-VLA vs. human tele-op orchestration. ER 2 outperforms ER 1.6 in all three modes, confirming the orchestration gain does not depend on any specific downstream controller.
  • Cross-embodiment On-Device 2 fine-tuning budget. The launch reports fast adaptation to Dexmate, SO101, and Trossen platforms with typically < 200 demonstrations and a few hours of training time, across bodies with drastically different shapes, sensors, and DoF counts — concrete evidence that motion transfer is a real transfer mechanism, not a re-training loop.

Safety & limitations

  • Named contribution: ASIMOV-Agentic benchmark — problem, mechanism, why.
    • Problem. Prior semantic-safety benchmarks (ASIMOV / ASIMOV-2) evaluated static text-in / text-out safety reasoning of an LLM. Real embodied agents need to (i) refuse unsafe tasks, (ii) trigger protective stops on hardware / proximity events, (iii) shield the VLA from infeasible or out-of-distribution requests, and (iv) proactively resolve ambiguous instructions or scene uncertainty by asking the human. There was no benchmark that measured all four in a single agentic protocol.
    • Mechanism. ASIMOV-Agentic contributes six evaluation components: Unsafe Task Refusal & Safety Constraint Following (Text / Pointing / Bounding-Box / Tool-use output modalities), Proactive Human Safety Monitoring (single-frame stereo + agentic tool-use variants), Safety Tool Calling (JSON safety-message handling under mid-task interruption), VLA Feasibility Awareness (one-step + multi-step VLA-confidence-emulation variant), Instruction Ambiguity & Requesting Human Clarification (10-class ambiguity taxonomy), and Aleatoric Scene Uncertainty (obfuscated instrument reading). Data is released on Hugging Face; each component ships with paired “good response” / “bad response” examples.
    • Why. The report is explicit that no current frontier model sits in the ideal top-right quadrant of the agentic human-proximity trade-off (near-zero false-negative rate and near-zero false-positive rate); the benchmark is designed to make this trade-off visible rather than to be immediately saturated. It also documents the tension between helpfulness (fulfil clear directives) and uncertainty awareness (intercept ambiguous ones), which appears in every component and which any deployed embodied agent has to navigate explicitly.
  • The safety report explicitly scopes itself to high-level semantic reasoning and orchestration and does not evaluate the underlying functional-safety architecture — certified hardware, redundancy, real-time system guarantees required for compliant deployment. Regulatory context is anchored in ISO 10218:2025, ISO/TS 15066, ISO 13482, the forthcoming ISO 25785-1 (dynamically stable industrial mobile robots), and IEC 60204-1 stop categories.
  • Privacy and fairness are called out as out of scope for the current ASIMOV-Agentic release; the report identifies demographic / geographic equity, cultural-context adaptation, and strict data minimization for private information as ongoing research directions.
  • The launch does not report SIM → real hallucination rates for the VLA under long-horizon deployment, or long-term reliability of On-Device 2 adaptation across weeks of use. The multi-finger dexterity numbers (screw bulb 36%, dustpan 32%) are labeled by DeepMind as a known limitation.
