AVA-Encoder: Towards Agent-Native Video Representation Learning

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-08-12 Video representation · Knowledge graph · Textual gradients · Creative agents

Overview

  • Chuyue Li, Jinpeng Yu, Haozhe Wang, Xueyun Tian, Zhijing Zhang, Bingnan Li, Shuqi Gu, Kan Ren, Jiaming Liu and Ruihua Hua (Qwen Business Unit, Alibaba, and collaborators; corresponding author Ruihua Hua) release AVA-Encoder, an Agentic Video Auto-Encoder that learns agent-native video representations by auto-encoding film into a typed knowledge graph and reconstructing it back to video. arXiv:2608.12313 [cs.CV], HF Daily 2026-08-19, submitted on the Qwen Business Unit HF org.
  • The problem framing: creative agents that generate video today have no structured representation of film that is both faithful to the source and directly editable by the agent. Raw pixel latents are opaque; caption strings lose scene structure; existing scene graphs miss modality assets. The paper's premise is that a representation an agent can query, edit, and reason over must jointly carry structured text, images, audio, and video, in a schema the model already knows how to manipulate.

Methodology

  • Named contribution 1 — knowledge-graph auto-encoding of film.
    • Problem. A video representation useful to a creative agent has to satisfy two conflicting requirements: it must be lossless enough that the source video can be reconstructed from it, and it must be structured enough that an agent can locate a specific shot, character, prop, or sound and modify it. Flat text descriptions and dense latents both fail the second requirement.
    • Mechanism. AVA-Encoder maps each video to a knowledge-graph representation with three node families: hierarchy nodes (film → scenes → shots) and state nodes (per-shot state descriptions) hold structured text; a linked asset layer holds generated images, audio, and video clips. Typed edges preserve the relations among these text descriptions and modality assets, so an agent's query — "the prop the protagonist is holding in scene 3 shot 2" — resolves through the graph to the right asset. Reconstruction runs the KG back through a decoder that renders shot-level video from the linked assets and state text.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Prior structured video representations either serialize to plain text (asset relations are lost) or to a scene graph without a linked modality layer (the agent can query structure but not manipulate audio/image assets in-place). Typing the edges and factoring the graph into hierarchy, state, and assets keeps every level of granularity addressable and every asset editable in the modality the downstream generator needs. This is what makes the representation "agent-native": the operations an editing agent already emits — "swap this asset," "rewrite this state" — land on the schema directly.
  • Named contribution 2 — textual-gradient optimization from reconstruction differences.
    • Problem. Training the encoding policy needs a differentiable signal, but the loss the paper cares about — the difference between the source video and the video reconstructed from the KG — is not straightforwardly differentiable with respect to the LLM prompt or the KG schema that produced it. Human labels are also expensive at film scale.
    • Mechanism. AVA-Encoder computes reconstruction differences (measured by an agentic video reconstruction benchmark released with the paper), then expresses those differences as natural-language update directions — textual gradients — that specify what the encoding policy should have done differently. Two loops consume these signals: Data-Independent Encoding Policy Pseudo-Training in the outer loop updates the encoding-policy prompt / behavior across a corpus of films, and Data-Dependent KG Representation Refinement in the optional test-time inner loop refines the KG representation for a specific target film.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Textual-gradient methods (in the style of DSPy / TextGrad) usually optimize a single prompt with respect to a scalar reward. Here the two-loop split lets the outer loop bake generic film-editing skill into the policy without depending on any particular target film's reconstruction, while the inner loop lets a deployment refine the KG for a specific film without retraining the encoder. That decoupling is what gives the abstract's 74.3% system-prompt-token reduction over a human-tuned policy: the outer loop absorbs most of the skill that would otherwise have to be spelled out in-prompt.

Evaluation & Results

  • Headline: on the released agentic video reconstruction benchmark AVA-Encoder improves by 20.7 percentage points over the strongest external baseline.
  • In the controlled policy-only setting, the pseudo-trained shot-level Agentic Video Encoder policy also outperforms a carefully human-tuned policy while using 74.3% fewer system-prompt tokens — a joint quality and prompt-length win, not a Pareto trade-off.
  • Availability: the paper releases the full AVA-Encoder framework, the agentic video reconstruction benchmark, and the first dataset of high-quality film KG representations (release channels enumerated in the paper).

StartupBench: Benchmarking General-Purpose Agents on Market-Validated End-to-End Workflows

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-08-18 Agent benchmark · End-to-end workflows · Rubric evaluation

Overview

  • Liya Zhu, Xin Ma, Tao Liu, Haodong Wang, Ge Zhang, Jingzhe Ding et al., 37 authors (ByteDance Seed and collaborators; corresponding author Wenhao Huang), release StartupBench, an end-to-end agent benchmark whose tasks are drawn from market-validated AI-startup product workflows rather than researcher intuition. arXiv:2608.17800 [cs.AI], HF Daily 2026-08-19, on the ByteDance Seed HF org.
  • The framing: existing agent benchmarks (WebArena-style, SWE-bench-style, AgentBench-style) fix tasks up-front from what researchers guess is useful, and improvements on them may not generalize to what real users pay AI systems to do. StartupBench's premise is that AI startups that have achieved market adoption are a filter for "tasks with demonstrated practical demand," and that translating their production workflows into deliverable-oriented tasks yields a benchmark whose signal actually tracks deployment value.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — market-validated E2E workflow benchmark.
    • Problem. A benchmark authored top-down from "capabilities researchers think agents should have" can miss the operational structure of tasks users actually pay to have completed — the multi-step deliverables, the domain-specific instructions, the tolerance for partial output. Progress on such benchmarks can saturate without moving the needle on deployment quality.
    • Mechanism. The construction pipeline: (a) enumerate AI startups with demonstrated product adoption; (b) study each product's actual user workflows and the professional domains its users come from; (c) translate each workflow into a complete deliverable-oriented task (produce artifact X given brief Y under constraints Z), not a single tool call; (d) author a fine-grained rubric per task that captures the workflow's real requirements (format, correctness, coverage, tone), not a boolean pass/fail. Evaluation puts every model behind a unified agent harness so the number reflects the model, not the scaffold.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Prior benchmarks either use synthetic tasks (WebArena, MiniWoB) or curated developer tasks (SWE-bench). Neither samples from the operational-workflow distribution that AI-product usage actually follows. Using market adoption as a task-selection filter shifts the distribution to tasks that survived a real user-value test, and rubric-based scoring surfaces partial progress that boolean scoring erases — which the paper reports as substantial across models, so a 30% completion rate coexists with much higher fractional progress and is informative about failure modes rather than opaque.

Evaluation & Results

  • Headline: across the representative models the paper evaluates under its unified harness, the strongest agent completes only approximately 30% of StartupBench tasks. Substantial partial progress is measurable across many tasks, so the gap is on the last-mile deliverable rather than on getting started.
  • Failure-mode analysis identifies complex instruction following (rubrics that combine multiple binding constraints) and domain-specific expertise (workflows in specialist professional domains) as the primary sources of failure.
  • Specific per-model breakdown (which frontier models, per-domain scores) is in the paper body; the abstract reports only the aggregate ceiling.

Other

  • Positioning: the paper argues StartupBench should be read as "an empirical measure of progress toward E2E completions of real-world user tasks" rather than a leaderboard-style capability benchmark; the intended use is diagnosing where general-purpose agents fall short of product-grade autonomy.

MoE-ViE: Mixture of Experts Vision Encoder for Efficient Image and Video Understanding

Tier 2 · Research Paper (ECCV 2026) arXiv 2026-08-18 Vision encoder · MoE · CLIP · Auxiliary-loss-free balancing · Frame distillation

Overview

  • Bonan Zhang, Shiyu Dong, Quan Hung Tran, Katharina Gschwind, Shuqi Yang, Sijia Chen, Adel Ahmadyan, Seungwhan Moon, Lu Zhang, Ahmed Kirmani, Babak Damavandi and Anuj Kumar (AI at Meta / Meta Superintelligence Labs) present MoE-ViE, a mixture-of-experts CLIP-style vision encoder family designed to scale encoder capacity without paying dense-scaling compute or latency. arXiv:2608.17402 [cs.CV]; accepted to ECCV 2026; code at facebookresearch/moe_vie. HF Daily 2026-08-19.
  • The framing: MoE has been the dominant capacity-scaling recipe in LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, GLM-5, Kimi K3, Qwen3.8-Max), but the MoE design space for CLIP-style vision encoders was not systematically explored at SOTA scale. Dense vision-encoder scaling still incurs the full compute and inference-latency bill; the paper's premise is that fine-grained MoE topologies can close that gap.

Methodology

  • Named contribution 1 — fine-grained MoE topology for CLIP-style vision encoders.
    • Problem. Naively porting standard LLM-style MoE (a small number of large experts per FFN, coarse routing) to a vision encoder underuses the sparsity headroom: coarse routing does not exploit the patch-token diversity a vision encoder sees, and yields modest gains over dense scaling at SOTA.
    • Mechanism. The paper sweeps the MoE topology axes systematically — number of experts, expert width, top-k routing granularity, layer placement — and lands on a fine-grained configuration (many smaller experts, finer routing) that consistently outperforms both dense and standard MoE counterparts across a range of encoder sizes.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Vision patch tokens vary substantially in content (background, object edges, text, texture), so many narrow experts specialized per content type extract more signal from a fixed activated-parameter budget than a handful of wide experts. Standard LLM-style MoE assumes token-level linguistic variability that is denser than the vision-token distribution and thus over-provisions per expert; fine-grained MoE fits the vision case better.
  • Named contribution 2 — auxiliary-loss-free load balancing.
    • Problem. Standard MoE trains with an auxiliary load-balancing loss to keep experts utilized, but that auxiliary term competes with the primary objective and can leave utilization uneven or hurt loss.
    • Mechanism. MoE-ViE uses a balancing variant that does not add an auxiliary loss term — the balancing signal is applied via routing bias / gate adjustment (analogous to the DeepSeek-V3-style loss-free balancer) so that expert utilization is kept even without interfering with the primary CLIP objective.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The auxiliary-loss approach couples two objectives with a hyperparameter (loss weight) that has to be tuned; too high hurts task loss, too low leaves imbalance. Adjusting the router's bias directly at the routing decision solves balance where the imbalance actually shows up, without pushing on the main gradient.
  • Named contribution 3 — specialized MoE kernel for inference latency.
    • Problem. A fine-grained MoE inflates the count of kernel launches per token (one per expert selection) and multiplies memory traffic; naive kernels erase the FLOP savings from sparse activation with launch and memory overhead, so wall-clock inference latency ends up worse than the dense baseline.
    • Mechanism. The paper ships a specialized MoE kernel that mitigates this inference latency overhead. Exact kernel details are in the paper body; the abstract commits to the outcome — the released encoder can be served at wall-clock latency the fine-grained MoE topology would otherwise not sustain.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Fusing the expert dispatch and expert compute for many small experts avoids per-expert launches and interleaves memory transfers with compute; without this, the fine-grained topology is a training-time win only, and the latency claim in the abstract (matching a SOTA encoder 1.7× its size at 76% of its latency) does not hold in the wild.
  • Named contribution 4 — frame-level distillation with a freezing mechanism.
    • Problem. Extending an image encoder to video usually improves video benchmarks but degrades the image benchmarks the encoder was pretrained on, because video training data shifts the encoder's representation toward temporal / motion features and away from static object semantics.
    • Mechanism. MoE-ViE distills at the frame level (each video frame is treated as a distillation target) paired with a novel freezing mechanism that keeps the components carrying image knowledge from drifting during video adaptation. The freezing is applied selectively; the abstract does not name which parameters or experts are frozen, but the design intent is that the video adaptation cannot backpropagate into the components that hold image capability.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Prior "image-then-video" recipes either full-finetune (losing image quality) or LoRA on video (limited video capacity). Frame-level distillation plus targeted freezing decouples the two capabilities: video training gets to update the parts that need to learn motion, while the image-capability substrate is protected by construction.

Evaluation & Results

  • Zero-shot: MoE-ViE is pretrained across a range of sizes and every size consistently outperforms its dense counterpart. The largest MoE-ViE matches the zero-shot performance of a SOTA encoder 1.7× its size, at 76% of that encoder's latency.
  • Aligned with an LLM: MoE-ViE surpasses all compared encoders on image and video benchmarks, including encoders with up to 5× more activated parameters. Specific per-benchmark numbers are in the paper body.
  • Availability: facebookresearch/moe_vie.

Agent Lightning v1.0: Towards Harnessed Agentic RL

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-08-18 Agentic RL · Harness · SWE-bench · Post-training framework

Overview

  • Zhiyuan He, Siwei Zhang, Zhiwen Zhou, Yuqing Yang, Yu Kang, Yuge Zhang, Luna K. Qiu, Tin Yan Tsui, Jiahang Xu and Chong Luo (Microsoft; corresponding author Chong Luo) release Agent Lightning v1.0, a lightweight framework for what the paper coins harnessed agentic RL. arXiv:2608.17528 [cs.AI], 2026-08-18 submission; HF Daily 2026-08-19.
  • The framing: modern deployed agents run inside an agent harness that manages tools, context, and control flow (LangGraph, verl Uni-Agent, AReaL 2.0, slime, Polar, and the paper's own original Agent Lightning). If the harness is what actually runs at inference time, then post-training the agent's LLM without the harness in the loop optimizes against the wrong environment. Harnessed agentic RL puts the deploy-time harness in the training loop — the harness owns the environment interaction, the trainer only observes the LLM's request-response pairs — and Agent Lightning v1.0 is the reference implementation, plus a study of the technical challenges this creates.

Methodology

  • Named contribution 1 — harnessed agentic RL paradigm and disaggregated architecture.
    • Problem. Traditional agentic RL bakes the environment loop into the trainer: the RL framework calls the environment, receives observations, and steps the LLM. This tightly couples the training stack to the specific harness used at inference, and every new agent harness (each with its own tool-calling schema, memory manager, control flow) demands a bespoke integration.
    • Mechanism. The prior work introduced a disaggregated architecture that decouples the two sides: the harness runs as it would in production and issues LLM calls through a proxied LLM endpoint. The trainer sits behind that endpoint. From the harness's point of view it is calling a normal LLM; from the trainer's point of view it observes only a stream of LLM request-response pairs. Any harness that speaks the LLM-endpoint protocol trains under the same code path. v1.0 formalizes this as the harnessed agentic RL paradigm, and the same design has since been adopted by verl Uni-Agent, AReaL 2.0, slime, and Polar.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The invariant the paper enforces — the trainer's view is a sequence of LLM request-response pairs — is precisely the invariant across harnesses. Trainer-agnostic harness support falls out of it. It also makes the deployment / training gap small: the same harness that scores at inference is the one that generated the trajectories.
  • Named contribution 2 — treatment of request-response-only supervision.
    • Problem. When the trainer only sees LLM request-response pairs (not the environment or the raw trajectory), a stack of concrete technical issues appears that traditional agentic RL sidesteps because it owns the loop: retokenization (each request is tokenized independently and their concatenation may not match the harness's runtime tokenization), sample merging (multi-turn traces are made of many request-response pairs that must be aggregated into a single training sample), advantage calculation (per-token vs. per-turn credit, and how to attribute reward across a sequence of harness calls), loss normalization (across turns of different lengths, and across samples containing different call counts), and backend scheduling (the proxy has to schedule inference against training I/O without stalling either side).
    • Mechanism. Agent Lightning v1.0 is presented as a testbed for these five challenges. Each is a concrete design decision inside the ~3,500-line framework; specific choices (which tokenizer normalization, which merging rule, which advantage formulation, which normalization scheme, which scheduler) are itemized in the paper body. The abstract emphasizes that these decisions substantially affect training stability and effectiveness, i.e. they are not implementation trivia.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Making the invariant "trainer sees request-response pairs" the interface means these five issues are the whole surface area of harnessed agentic RL, and once solved once, they apply to every harness that plugs in. The paper positions the framework as reference-implementation-plus-testbed so that later systems (or successor frameworks) can substitute alternative solutions and compare.
  • Named contribution 3 — reproducible coding-agent RL pipeline.
    • Problem. Published SWE-bench RL results are hard to reproduce because the environment (containers, tool stack, patch application, test harness) is not always released, and the RL recipe (reward shaping, on-policy vs. off-policy, rollouts per step) is often abbreviated.
    • Mechanism. The paper ships a complete reproducible pipeline for coding-agent RL under Agent Lightning v1.0: the harness (tools, control flow), the training data, the RL loop, and the eval. The recipe drives Qwen3.5-9B on SWE-bench Verified from 41.8% to 56.4% using only 6K training examples and modest compute.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The +14.6-point absolute gain from 6K examples is the paper's headline evidence that harnessed agentic RL is data-efficient when the deploy harness participates in training: gradients flow into behaviors the harness actually calls at inference, not proxies for them. Prior agentic RL results on coding often required larger data budgets or full-repo self-play to move the number, and were tied to a specific in-house harness.

Evaluation & Results

  • Coding: Qwen3.5-9B, 41.8% → 56.4% on SWE-bench Verified with 6K training examples and modest compute (+14.6 points absolute).
  • Evaluation surface includes instruction-following and search agents in addition to coding; per-benchmark numbers on those two are in the paper body.
  • Framework: ~3,500 lines of code. Advertised property is arbitrary-harness support: the LLM-endpoint proxy protocol is the extension seam.

Other

  • Availability: full workflow and training scripts released; the paper explicitly positions the release as reproducibility infrastructure for the harnessed agentic RL line of work.
  • Positioning against ecosystem: the paper claims the disaggregated LLM-endpoint approach it originated has since been picked up by verl Uni-Agent, AReaL 2.0, slime, and Polar, and frames v1.0 as the reference-implementation of the pattern rather than a new competing framework.

CoinVE-200K: A Large-Scale High-Quality Dataset for Compositional Instruction-Guided Video Editing

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-08-18 Video editing · Compositional instructions · Region attention · Dataset · Benchmark

Overview

  • Fuchen Long, Cong Wang, Zitao Gao, Wenhao Zhong, Yu Cheng, Xiaolu Hou, Yan Li, Xiao Cao, Xinlong Sun, Xi Chen and Yu Liu (Tencent) release CoinVE-200K, a large-scale, high-quality dataset for compositional instruction-guided video editing; CoinVE-Bench, the paired benchmark; and CoinVE-Edit, a 22B compositional video editor. arXiv:2608.17566 [cs.CV]; project page coinve200k.github.io; dataset at huggingface.co/datasets/FireCRT/CoinVE-200K; source at coinve200k/CoinVE-200K. HF Daily 2026-08-19.
  • The framing: existing instruction-based video-editing datasets focus on a single editing operation per sample (change color, remove object, restyle). Real editing prompts are compositional — "put a hat on the person on the left, remove the car in the background, and stylize the whole thing as watercolor" — and require multiple intents to be jointly parsed and jointly executed on distinct regions of the same video, preserving irrelevant content and temporal coherence throughout.

Methodology

  • Named contribution 1 — CoinVE-200K dataset construction pipeline.
    • Problem. There is no public compositional video-editing corpus at the scale and resolution needed to train and evaluate compositional editors: existing datasets either give a single edit per sample or fail on temporal consistency / instruction faithfulness at 1080p and multi-second clips.
    • Mechanism. CoinVE-200K contains 1080p video-editing pairs of up to 201 frames, with each sample carrying 2–5 atomic editing operations composed into a single instruction. Instructions target humans, objects, and backgrounds, and cover addition, removal, modification, and stylization. A dedicated generation-and-filtering pipeline enforces four axes at construction time: instruction faithfulness (the edited video actually reflects each atomic op), visual quality (no artifacts), temporal consistency (no flicker between edited and unedited regions), and compositional diversity (no over-representation of a small set of op combinations).
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Prior single-op datasets teach an editor to execute one intent at a time; a model trained on them at compositional test time must generalize compositionally with no supervision. Filtering compositional samples up-front on faithfulness, quality, consistency, and diversity gives the training signal the compositional structure directly, and the 1080p / 201-frame scale removes the resolution and length gap between training data and real editing use.
  • Named contribution 2 — CoinVE-Bench evaluation surface.
    • Problem. Compositional editing has no established benchmark that varies subject type, operation type, and instruction complexity together; scoring on single-op benchmarks masks the specific failure mode of compositional editors (correct on some atomic ops, wrong on others in the same sample, or global temporal breakage).
    • Mechanism. CoinVE-Bench scores compositional-instruction video editing across three axes — subject (human / object / background), operation type (add / remove / modify / stylize), and instruction complexity (op count and dependence structure) — and reports the metrics the dataset filter itself uses: instruction following, compositional editing accuracy, visual quality, temporal consistency.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Because the benchmark axes match the training-set filter axes, a per-axis score decomposes failure into "which subject, which op type, which complexity level" — the diagnostic granularity a compositional-editing paper actually needs.
  • Named contribution 3 — CoinVE-Edit and region-disentangled attention.
    • Problem. A single global cross-attention between instruction text and video features cannot easily separate "put a hat on the left person" from "remove the car in the background" — both attend to the same latent frame, and their gradients interfere, so multi-region compositional edits leak into irrelevant content or into each other.
    • Mechanism. CoinVE-Edit is 22B, built on Wan2.1-T2V-14B as the video backbone and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct as the multimodal instruction encoder. Its region-disentangled attention splits the composite instruction into atomic-op sub-instructions and gives each one its own attention path over the corresponding region of the video features; irrelevant regions are protected from that path. The disentangled attentions are then fused into a single denoising step over the video latent.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Disentangling attention by region avoids the interference that global cross-attention pays whenever two intents target different regions of the same video — each intent's gradient signal reaches only the region it edits, so preservation of irrelevant content and temporal coherence outside the edited regions follows almost by construction. The alternative (a compositional planner that runs edits sequentially) accumulates temporal-consistency error across passes; single-pass disentangled attention avoids that pileup.

Evaluation & Results

  • On CoinVE-Bench, CoinVE-Edit posts strong performance in instruction following, compositional editing accuracy, visual quality, and temporal consistency. Per-axis and per-baseline numbers are in the paper body; the abstract does not table them against a closed frontier video editor.
  • Dataset scale: 200K pairs, 1080p, up to 201 frames per pair, 2–5 atomic ops per sample.
  • Availability: dataset on Hugging Face, code on GitHub, project page with samples.
