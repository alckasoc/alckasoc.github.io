Qwen-Audio-3.1-Realtime: Reliable Agentic Voice via Think/Act/Speak-and-Coordinate

Tier 1 · Technical Report + Managed API Launch arXiv 2026-09-28 Voice model launch · Full-duplex · Tool-use RL · Multilingual speech

Overview

  • Alibaba Token Foundry (formerly Qwen team) shipped Qwen-Audio-3.1-Realtime on 2026-09-28: API-only via QwenCloud WebSocket as qwen-audio-3.1-realtime-plus, no open weights. Companion release Qwen-Audio-3.1-ASR-Flash-Filetrans is an offline transcription model with hot-word support, speaker separation, multilingual and Chinese dialect ASR at $0.15/$0.47 per M input/output.
  • Positioning: reliable agentic voice interaction. The report frames prior real-time voice models as either half-duplex (missing turn-taking) or full-duplex-but-fragile (fillers, background-speech false replies, weak tool use). v3.1's pitch is that it holds full-duplex turn-taking while adding executable tool-call reliability and multilingual ASR gains.
  • Pricing (per M tokens): audio input $6.4; text input $0.8; combined text-and-audio output $24 (output text is not charged separately). Context window 262K total (245K max input + 16K max output). Function calling, web search, structured outputs, context cache, fine-tuning supported. 14 languages covered in the multilingual benchmark battery.

Methodology

  • Two-model architecture sharing an Audio Encoder + LLM design. A full-duplex decision model processes streaming audio and decides at each step whether to listen, speak, stop, or resume. A speech-to-text model generates the response content as text on the same audio input. Text is then converted to streaming speech by a context-aware voice renderer that uses conversation history and acoustic context. The two models share the same audio encoder and language-model backbone; only heads differ.
  • Named contribution — M2-OPD (Multimodality and Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation, "Think" layer).
    • Problem. An audio-native LM has to inherit a text LM's reasoning and knowledge without an intermediate transcription step, and it has to absorb specialised audio understanding (emotion, pragmatic intent, acoustic scenes) without splintering into a separate model per skill.
    • Mechanism. M2-OPD runs two on-policy distillation stages on student-generated trajectories. In Multimodality OPD, the Audio Student policy is supervised by a Text Teacher scoring student prefixes on text-equivalent prompts, plus a frozen Audio Reference giving audio-conditioned distributions on the same contexts — the student pulls text-LM behavior across through the Text Teacher term and audio grounding through the reference term. In Multi-Teacher OPD, domain-specific audio experts (each independently trained via GRPO on emotion, pragmatic intent, or acoustic-scene tasks) supervise the main model's own trajectories at the token level, consolidating specialist behavior into one deployable AudioLM. Trained on approximately 1M hours of paired audio-text data spanning audio understanding, spoken instruction following, multi-turn interaction, tool-oriented requests, emotion, empathy, pragmatic intent, and acoustic-scene understanding.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. On-policy distillation avoids the exposure-bias failure of teacher-forced SFT on paired transcripts (where the audio student's outputs during deployment drift out of the teacher's training distribution). Splitting supervision across a text teacher and a frozen audio reference is what lets the student absorb text-LM knowledge without losing audio conditioning; multi-teacher token-level distillation lets one deployable model carry several audio specialisations without model-per-skill routing.
  • Named contribution — Self-evolving executable environments with three validation gates ("Act" layer).
    • Problem. Voice-agent tool use requires the model to select a tool, ground its parameters, verify state, and report back — and existing tool-use RL work grades all of those under one trajectory-level reward, which conflates a wrong tool choice with a right tool call reported badly. Training environments also age out as tool contracts change.
    • Mechanism. A pipeline generates executable environments (tools, policies, databases, tasks) and admits each through three gates: a static gate replays a reference solution from initial database state and checks expected results, permitted writes, policy clauses, and information boundaries; a rollout gate evaluates model trajectories against database results, write operations, and behavioral requirements (confirmation, truthful status reporting); a difficulty gate runs each task multiple times and labels it as consistently solved, consistently failed, or boundary. Only boundary-case tasks feed into GRPO training, run at dialogue-, milestone-, and turn-level rollout granularities.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Separating tool selection, parameter grounding, state verification, and progress communication into gate-checked signals means GRPO's advantage can attribute credit to the right decision point rather than smearing over the whole rollout. Filtering to boundary tasks concentrates learning signal on rollouts where the model is uncertain, which is where policy-gradient methods extract the most information per step.
  • Named contribution — Full-duplex speak-and-coordinate policy.
    • Problem. Full-duplex speech agents fail in three characteristic ways: talking through user interruptions, replying to overheard background chatter, and emitting fillers ("um", "hmm") that read as hesitation. Prior full-duplex baselines respond to background speech ~73% of the time on Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5.
    • Mechanism. The Speak-and-Coordinate layer governs three orthogonal policy dimensions: how to speak (persona, empathy, expression constraints), when to speak (pause handling, turn-taking, interruption response), and whether to speak or act (tool triggers, verification requirements, capability boundaries). The full-duplex decision model gates output at millisecond scale on the acoustic stream, while the S2T path composes reply content independently.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Prior full-duplex systems couple speech-timing decisions to content generation, so a slow content stream produces false fillers. Decoupling the timing model from the content model lets the timing head suppress speech during background-only audio without waiting for the S2T path to finish, dropping background-speech response rate from 73.0% on v3.0 to 13.0% on v3.1 and cutting filler rate from 0.7590 to 0.2960 on v3.0-equivalent evaluations.

Simplex Diffusion Models: Discrete Diffusion on the Probability Simplex

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-28 Discrete diffusion · Language modeling · Distillation · Math reasoning

Overview

  • Justin Deschenaux, Alexandre Galashov, Andrew Campbell, Li Kevin Wenliang, James Thornton, Arnaud Doucet and Valentin De Bortoli at Google DeepMind, EPFL and UCL Gatsby introduce Simplex Diffusion Models (SDMs), a discrete diffusion formulation that operates on the probability simplex rather than on categorical samples. Frontier-lab affiliation is satisfied by the Google DeepMind author majority.
  • The claim: prior discrete diffusion approaches suffer from information collapse because each denoising step samples a categorical outcome and discards the model's uncertainty about which category is right. SDMs carry the full probability distribution across steps so uncertainty is preserved end-to-end.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Simplex Diffusion (main method).
    • Problem. Discrete diffusion (D3PM, MDLM, masked diffusion) at each reverse step samples a token from the current categorical distribution and moves on. If the model was 60% confident about the token, that 60%-40% uncertainty is lost the moment sampling happens; the next step conditions only on the sampled outcome. This information collapse makes low-step sampling brittle: distillation to 8 steps loses substantially more quality than the equivalent continuous diffusion, which passes real-valued means.
    • Mechanism. SDM lifts the reverse process onto the probability simplex: state at each denoising step is a probability distribution over the vocabulary rather than a categorical sample. The forward process is defined so that the reverse admits closed-form transitions — no ODE integration is needed, unlike Dirichlet Flow Matching. Training uses a plain cross-entropy loss between the model's predicted distribution and the target. Sampling uses a DDIM-like scheme with a tunable stochasticity dial: pure deterministic sampling for max efficiency, or a Langevin-style noise term for exploration.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Carrying probability mass across denoising steps means the reverse process has strictly more information at each step than a categorical-sampling reverse process would have. On distillation, this pays off: at 8 steps, SDMs solve 32.1% of GSM8K versus discrete diffusion's 21.4% at 128 steps (see Evaluation). Closed-form transitions and cross-entropy training are what make it a practical substitute for existing discrete diffusion pipelines: same objective, same computational profile, no ODE solver in the loop.

  • On code generation via TinyGSM, SDMs beat masked/uniform diffusion baselines by +3.2 pp at the same step count.

Ablations

  • The stochasticity dial of the DDIM-like sampler is exposed as an inference-time knob rather than fixed at training; the paper reports quality is stable across a wide range of noise multipliers, with pure deterministic sampling used for the low-step distilled runs.
  • Comparison to Dirichlet Flow Matching: SDM avoids the ODE-integration step that DFM requires, at matched or better quality on the tested benchmarks.

Other

  • Positioning within DeepMind's diffusion-LLM line: SDMs sit between masked-diffusion approaches (which are throwing away uncertainty by hard-masking) and continuous-embedding diffusion (which loses the discrete-token guarantees). The simplex formulation is the smallest change to standard discrete-diffusion pipelines that recovers the uncertainty-preservation of continuous-space diffusion.

How Far Are We from Removing the Visual Encoder? Scaling Laws for Encoder-Free Multimodal Pretraining

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-09-28 Multimodal scaling laws · Encoder-free MLLM · MoE decoder · IsoFLOP

Overview

  • Lin Chen (CASIA / UCAS, Tencent internship), Bolin Ni, Qi Yang, Lan Jiang, Kun Ding, Xiaoran Fan, Hower Yang, Ying Wang at Tencent Foundation Model Department, with Shiming Xiang at CASIA. Frontier-lab affiliation via the Tencent author majority.
  • The question the paper addresses: if a multimodal LLM feeds projected image patches directly into the decoder and asks the decoder to learn its own visual representations from pixels, how much extra compute does that cost versus a decoder-plus-frozen-encoder MLLM, and at what compute budget do the two architectures cross over?

Methodology

  • Setup. A controlled scaling study on matched sparse-MoE decoder ladders from 1.1B total / 71M active up to 44B total / 2.4B active parameters, run once with an encoder-free decoder (raw image patches → projection → decoder with bidirectional visual attention on image tokens, causal elsewhere) and once with an encoder-based decoder (SigLIP ViT + ConvPool adapter + projector → same decoder). Identical data mixture, optimization setup, and visual-token granularity across both arms. IsoFLOP profiles are fit by varying model size M at fixed compute C with D = C/M.
  • Named contribution — Compute-optimal allocation law for encoder-free MLLMs.
    • Problem. Compute-optimal scaling (Mopt ∝ Ca, Dopt ∝ Cb, a+b=1) has been derived for text-only and encoder-based multimodal LLMs, but not for encoder-free MLLMs where the decoder is doing the visual-representation job the encoder used to do. Without that law, practitioners have no principled way to allocate a compute budget between model size and data for encoder-free training.
    • Mechanism. The paper fits the standard IsoFLOP formulation — loss ℒ(M, D) = E + AM−α + BD−β, loss-compute frontier ℒ*(C) = E + KC−γ, with γ = αβ/(α+β) — separately for encoder-free and encoder-based decoders, on both text-only and multimodal validation objectives. Uncertainty is quantified via bootstrap resampling at 80% CI. Efficiency-gain metrics EGC (extra compute for equal loss) and EGM (extra FLOPs per token for equal loss) are defined and estimated over the fitted range and the extrapolation region.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The IsoFLOP methodology is unchanged; the contribution is that fitting it in matched pairs isolates the encoder's contribution to compute-optimal allocation and lets the paper report an interpretable delta (multimodal a shifts from 0.464 encoder-based to 0.570 encoder-free, an ~11% shift toward preferring model size over data). Prior encoder-free MLLM work (Fuyu, Chameleon, EVE, SOLO) reported single-scale ablations; this is the first controlled compute-frontier fit.

  • Topic-level crossover ordering (from earliest to latest): STEM (near parity already), Charts (rapid convergence), then GUI, OCR, Caption (later, proportional to how much low-level visual perception is required).

Ablations

  • Vision-specific decoder adaptations that emerge in the encoder-free arm without any encoder-side supervision:
    • Attention. Attention mass on visual tokens at layer 12 (8B model) climbs from 0.217 at training start to 0.645, matching the encoder-based model's steady-state ~0.645.
    • Layerwise processing. Visual representations diverge from raw inputs much earlier in encoder-free models; shallow decoder layers act as an implicit visual-encoding stage.
    • Expert routing. Visual-token routing concentrates with scale, consistent with specialist MoE experts adopting visual roles.
    • Bidirectional vs causal. Causal visual attention yields a = 0.557 vs bidirectional's 0.570 on the multimodal objective — the compute-allocation shift is architectural (encoder-free vs encoder-based), not an artifact of the attention mask.
  • Robustness analyses: validation-curve envelope comparison, extrapolation-error analysis, sensitivity to the irreducible-loss parameter E, bootstrap intervals at 80% CI.
  • Encoder compute accounting: including the visual encoder's FLOPs in the encoder-based budget strengthens the encoder-free relative efficiency — the crossover budgets above use the more conservative decoder-only accounting.

Other

  • Limitations flagged by the authors: extrapolation past the measured 1019–1021 FLOP range assumes the fitted scaling law persists; encoder size is held fixed across decoder scales, potentially disadvantaging the encoder-based arm; crossover timing varies by task type so aggregate predictions mask heterogeneous behavior; sharp early-training loss drops in encoder-free models hint at optimization dynamics not fully characterised; results are limited to sparse-MoE decoders and generalisation to dense architectures is untested.
  • Practical implication: if the crossover is correct at ~6×1021–1×1022 FLOPs, then labs already training in the 1022–1024 FLOP range should be indifferent-to-preferring encoder-free multimodal training, and the specific advantage compounds as compute-per-token FLOP-efficiency EGM at ~0.80 means encoder-based models leave FLOPs on the table at fixed decoder size.
