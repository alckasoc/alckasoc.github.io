Google DeepMind

Introducing Gemini 3.6 Flash, 3.5 Flash-Lite, and 3.5 Flash Cyber

Tier 1 · Blog Launch + Companion Model Cards blog.google · deepmind.google 2026-07-21 Flagship Flash tier · Agentic · Computer use · Cybersecurity · Cost efficiency

Overview

  • Authors / sources: Introducing Gemini 3.6 Flash, 3.5 Flash-Lite, and 3.5 Flash Cyber by Tulsee Doshi (Sr. Director, PM, on behalf of the Gemini team) on the Google Keyword blog, plus the DeepMind companion post Introducing Gemini 3.5 Flash Cyber by Raluca Ada Popa and Four Flynn, and standalone 3.6 Flash and 3.5 Flash-Lite model cards. Affiliation: Google DeepMind and Google Cloud Vulnerability Research.
  • Central claim: the Flash tier is the correct optimization target for the current agentic-workload regime, and the correct move at that tier is efficiency — fewer output tokens, fewer reasoning steps, fewer tool calls, at lower price — while still improving raw benchmark scores. The three-model release is structured to cover the three main Flash use cases: 3.6 Flash as the workhorse with better coding / knowledge work / multimodal; 3.5 Flash-Lite as the throughput / cost anchor; 3.5 Flash Cyber as a domain specialization for CodeMender-driven vulnerability workflows.
  • Coordinated launch group with Gemini 3.5 Pro flagged as “currently testing with partners” and Gemini 4 flagged as having begun its most ambitious pre-training run yet. This is Google’s first coordinated Flash-tier triad since the 3 Flash generation.
  • Deployment (all live July 21): 3.6 Flash in Gemini API via Google AI Studio, Android Studio, Google Antigravity, Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, and the Gemini Enterprise / Gemini app; 3.5 Flash-Lite in Gemini API + rolling out to Google Search; 3.5 Flash Cyber exclusively inside CodeMender under a limited-access pilot for governments and trusted partners.

Architecture and positioning

  • Three-model Flash triad (family-level design). Problem. Production agentic workloads want throughput and cost-per-completed-task, not raw intelligence per token; a single Flash checkpoint can’t simultaneously optimize high-throughput low-latency subagent calls, workhorse multi-step multimodal reasoning, and specialized security-agent inner loops. Mechanism. Google splits the tier into three checkpoints with a shared 3.5-family lineage. 3.6 Flash is the workhorse, trained to spend fewer output tokens per completed task and take fewer tool calls in multi-step workflows. 3.5 Flash-Lite holds fixed the intelligence class of 3.5 Flash but strips latency to 350 output tokens/second and exposes controllable thinking levels (minimal / low / higher) so developers can pick throughput vs. reasoning per call. 3.5 Flash Cyber is a fine-tuned derivative of 3.5 Flash targeting CodeMender’s many-parallel-call security workflows. Why. Splitting the tier lets each checkpoint move further along its preferred axis than a single one-size-fits-all Flash could. The published proof is that 3.5 Flash-Lite at 350 tok/s and $0.30/M input beats the previous-mainline 3 Flash on both SWE-Bench Pro (54.2% vs 49.6%) and OSWorld-Verified (74.0% vs 65.1%) — Lite is no longer a compromise SKU.
  • Computer use as a built-in client-side tool (new deployment surface). Problem. Prior computer-use behavior in Gemini 3.5 Flash required custom orchestration; agents that need to click, type, and read the OS surface were building the tool integration themselves. Mechanism. Both 3.6 Flash and 3.5 Flash-Lite ship with computer use as a first-class tool in the Gemini API and Gemini Enterprise, with the tool executed client-side. Why. Making computer use a first-class tool removes a major source of integration variance and lets Google measure OSWorld-Verified against a canonical harness across releases (3.6 Flash: 83.0%; 3.5 Flash-Lite: 74.0%).
  • Enhanced Frontier Safety safeguards (3.6 Flash). Problem. Prior Frontier Safety Framework versions traded resistance to CBRN / cyber-offense jailbreaks against over-refusal on beneficial workloads. Mechanism. 3.6 Flash ships with enhanced CBRN and cyber-offense safeguards inside the Frontier Safety Framework, and the safety-training recipe explicitly minimizes refusals for beneficial uses. See the 3.6 Flash model card for the framework detail. Why. The refusal-minimization goal is the direct answer to the customer-side headache visible in 3.5 Flash Cyber’s own benchmarks: Google reports that Opus 4.6-and-later Claude releases now refuse the Chrome-production-commit-scanning task under safety guardrails, so the Chrome-commit chart cannot compare against them at all.

Pricing and efficiency

  • 3.6 Flash pricing: $1.50/M input, $7.50/M output (previous 3.5 Flash output: $9.00/M). This is a 17% price cut on output alone, compounding with the 17%-fewer-output-tokens efficiency reported on the Artificial Analysis Index to lower effective cost-per-completed-task by more than 30% on typical multi-step workloads (Google reports up to 65% savings on DeepSWE by Datacurve).
  • 3.5 Flash-Lite pricing: $0.30/M input, $2.50/M output, and 350 output tok/s per the Artificial Analysis Index. The reported step-up over 3.1 Flash-Lite is significant: Terminal-Bench 2.1 54% vs 31%, GDM-MRCR v2 long-context 72.2% vs 60.1%, GDPval-AA v2 1140 vs 642.
  • Both 3.6 Flash and 3.5 Flash-Lite retain the 3.5-family 1M-token input context window and 64K-token maximum output limit, native multimodal input (text / image / video / audio), and thinking controls. Knowledge cutoff advances from January 2025 (in 3.5 Flash) to March 2026.
  • Efficiency is not marketing-only: Google publishes a specific concrete claim — 3.6 Flash “takes fewer reasoning steps and tool calls to accomplish multi-step workflows” — and the DeepSWE (Datacurve) improvement of +12 points is delivered under the same harness that produced the previous 3.5 Flash baseline, so the delta is attributable to the model, not to prompting or scaffolding.

Mage-Flow: An Efficient Native-Resolution Foundation Model for Image Generation and Editing

Tier 1 · Technical Report arXiv 2026-07-21 Text-to-image · Instruction editing · Diffusion transformer · VAE · Distillation · RL alignment

Overview

  • Authors: Xinjie Zhang, Peng Zhang, Shicheng Zheng, Jinghao Guo, Zhaoyang Jia, Yifei Shen, Xun Guo, Yuxuan Luo, Jiahao Li, Wenxuan Xie, Fanyi Pu, Xiaoyi Zhang, Kaichen Zhang, Zongyu Guo, Tianci Bi, Dongnan Gui, Zhening Liu, Zimo Wen, Zihan Zheng, Senqiao Yang, Xiao Li, Jinglu Wang, Bin Li, Yan Lu (24 authors). Affiliation: Microsoft Mage Team. Project: microsoft.github.io/Mage; code: github.com/microsoft/Mage; weights: microsoft/Mage HuggingFace collection (microsoft/Mage-Flow, microsoft/Mage-Flow-Base, and 10 more).
  • Central claim: careful tokenizer–backbone–system co-design at compact 4B scale can match or beat open-source image generation and editing systems 5–20× its size (Z-Image 6B, Qwen-Image 20B, FLUX.2 32B, Hunyuan-Image-3.0 80B, FireRed-Image-Edit 20B) while producing images fast enough for interactive desktop use. This is deliberately framed against the “open weights but not practical to deploy” gap in recent open-source image generation.
  • The Mage-Flow stack is one shared design instantiated into two families — Mage-Flow for text-to-image and Mage-Flow-Edit for instruction-based editing — each with Base, Diffusion-NFT-aligned, and 4-step Turbo variants. Base and Turbo weights are already live on HuggingFace as of July 21.
  • Reported flagship results at 1024² on a single NVIDIA A100 with peak memory 18–20 GB: Mage-Flow generates in 4.37s; Mage-Flow-Turbo in 0.59s; Mage-Flow-Edit edits in 10.55s (30 steps); Mage-Flow-Edit-Turbo in 1.02s. Native-resolution support spans 512–2048 in each dimension including 4:1 aspect ratios like 512×2048 and 2048×512.

Architecture

  • Mage-VAE: one-step diffusion-style tokenizer with anchor-latent KL (named contribution). Problem. Modern latent generative pipelines invoke the VAE during training, inference, and repeated editing, but the VQGAN- and LDM-derived architectures inherited by SD-VAE, SD-3.5-VAE, FLUX-VAE, and FLUX.2-VAE were designed for 256² images. At 1K and 2K, they use global attention and heavy high-resolution blocks whose per-pixel MACs grow unfavorably — in FLUX.2-Klein-4B’s 4-step generation, the VAE decoder accounts for 14% of end-to-end time. Naively swapping in a smaller tokenizer breaks the diffusion backbone’s latent geometry. Mechanism. Mage-VAE is trained from scratch under three principles. (1) The decoder is a fully convolutional one-step pixel-diffusion model derived from CoD-Lite, pre-trained with a compression-oriented objective and distilled to one step — no global attention, no multi-step decode. (2) The encoder is the architectural dual: a one-step diffusion model that generates latents conditioned on pixels, making encoding as cheap as decoding. (3) The standard Gaussian-prior KL is replaced with an anchor-latent KL that regularizes the posterior toward the latent distribution of FLUX.2-VAE, producing a generation-ready latent space with 16× spatial reduction and 128 channels (internalizing the 2× patchification that FLUX.2-VAE requires downstream). Training runs in three stages: multi-step flow-matching pre-training, one-step decoder distillation with DMD + compression-teacher + DINOv2-projected GAN losses, and joint encoder/decoder fine-tuning with the anchor-latent KL. Why. Compression-oriented pre-training is empirically the right transfer objective for lightweight learned tokenizers (per CoD-Lite); the dual-symmetric encoder amortizes the tokenizer cost across both training and iterative editing; and the anchor-latent KL is what lets Mage-VAE be a drop-in replacement for FLUX.2-VAE in an already-trained backbone like FLUX.2-Klein-4B without regressing GenEval / DPG-Bench / TIIF / GEdit / TextEdit scores. The result: reconstruction PSNR 36.61 on CLIC 2020 (vs FLUX.2-VAE at 36.88) and 40.67 on FFHQ 1024² (vs 40.47) at ∼12.3× fewer encoding and ∼22.3× fewer decoding MACs per pixel.
  • Native-Resolution MMDiT (NR-MMDiT) with native-resolution packing (named contribution). Problem. Conventional MMDiT training bucket-quantizes the resolution distribution: each optimization step is restricted to samples from a single fixed resolution/aspect-ratio bucket, so the model learns a discretized subset of real image sizes and struggles with extreme aspect ratios unless dedicated buckets are added. Mechanism. NR-MMDiT is a 4B SD3-style joint-self-attention MMDiT with modality-specific normalization and projection for text and image streams. Instead of buckets, images with different resolutions and aspect ratios are Mage-VAE-encoded, flattened into variable-length latent-token sequences, and packed with variable-length text-token sequences into a single contiguous batch under a fixed token budget. FlashAttention’s variable-length kernels handle intra-sample attention via per-sample cumulative offsets, avoiding explicit block-diagonal masks; per-sample 2D RoPE embeds each image’s native spatial layout. The same packing trick is reused for CFG: conditional and unconditional branches pack into one forward pass, saving 1.09×–1.15× per denoising step without changing the denoising trajectory. Why. Native-resolution packing turns resolution diversity into a training signal — every update mixes heterogeneous image sizes, so a single checkpoint generalizes to 512–2048 on any aspect ratio, including the 4:1 extremes. The paper credits this design for the fact that Mage-Flow-Turbo at 4B beats FLUX.2-Klein-4B at the same parameter count on TIIF-Short (83.58 vs 77.40) and TIIF-Long (84.16 vs 79.43) with the exact same VAE.
  • Text conditioning: frozen Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct text encoder producing contextual embeddings that are packed with the visual tokens.
  • Editing conditioning (Mage-Flow-Edit): shared Mage-VAE latent space and NR-MMDiT backbone, but the conditioning is extended with editing instructions and source-image latents so the same architecture handles semantic content editing, appearance transformation, image restoration, and structure-aware outputs.

Pre-training

  • Data pipeline. Text-to-image data is filtered with sample-level quality signals, cross-sample deduplication, multi-granularity captioning (short + long captions per image), and concept-aware synthesis / balancing to correct long-tail concept distributions. Image-editing data is synthesized programmatically, VLM-filtered against edit-fidelity criteria, and tagged / balanced by edit type (semantic content, appearance transformation, restoration, structure-aware) so that no single edit type dominates.
  • Backbone pre-training. Mage-Flow-Base is trained from scratch as a 4B native-resolution foundation model with rectified flow-matching in the Mage-VAE latent space. Every optimization step processes a packed batch of variable-length latent-token sequences under a fixed token budget (paper reports 50,000 tokens per packed sample, 8-GPU B200 node in the training-efficiency ablation).
  • Editing pre-training. Mage-Flow-Edit-Base reuses the same latent space and backbone, extending the conditioning to include editing instructions and source-image latents. Editing pre-training mixes editing data with generation data so the model retains an open-ended generative prior while learning source-conditioned editability — the paper reports this data mix improves editing fidelity without hurting text-to-image quality.

Post-training

  • Diffusion-NFT alignment (Base → RL-aligned). Problem. Rectified-flow generators are hard to RL-align: their ODE sampler is deterministic and lacks an explicit action distribution, so DPO/PPO objectives don’t apply directly, and prior fixes like Flow-GRPO have to convert the ODE into a stochastic SDE. Mechanism. Mage-Flow adopts Diffusion-NFT: instead of RL over reverse-process denoising, it performs negative-aware fine-tuning on the forward process, requiring neither likelihood estimation nor a specific ODE solver. Applied to capability-targeted generation data, it targets prompt following, aesthetics, bilingual text rendering, and preference alignment. Applied to editing data, it targets editing fidelity. Why. Forward-process negative-aware alignment sidesteps the solver-lock-in and sampling-mode-limitation of SDE-conversion approaches like Flow-GRPO, and preserves the deterministic ODE sampler at inference — important because it’s the same sampler that the Turbo distillation targets.
  • Decoupled-DMD few-step distillation with adversarial perceptual guidance (RL-aligned → Turbo). Problem. Turbo variants have to reach 4-step generation quality without collapsing to bland or texture-poor images — DMD alone tends to lose visual detail at very small step counts. Mechanism. Mage-Flow-Turbo uses Decoupled-DMD, which separates CFG augmentation from distribution matching (CFG augmentation as the “spear,” distribution matching as the “shield”) and allows independent noise schedules for the two objectives. On top of that, SenseFlow-style adversarial perceptual guidance from frozen VFMs (CLIP + DINOv2) provides feature-space discriminator gradients to preserve semantic alignment and realism at low step counts. Why. The decoupling stabilizes distillation at high CFG scales (which the base Mage-Flow relies on for prompt adherence), and VFM-based adversarial guidance is stronger than pixel-space adversarial losses at preserving semantic identity at 4 steps.

  • The FLUX.2-VAE anchor-latent dependency means Mage-Flow’s generation quality is upper-bounded by the FLUX.2 latent geometry — if FLUX.2-VAE releases a substantially different latent standard, Mage-VAE would need re-alignment.

Availability

  • Base and Turbo weights for Mage-Flow and Mage-Flow-Edit live on the microsoft/Mage HuggingFace collection (12 models linked from the paper page). Code + inference scripts at github.com/microsoft/Mage. HuggingFace Space demo: hugging-apps/mage-flow.

Two-Level Meta-Rubrics for Evaluating Open-Ended Generation: GAMUT, a Benchmark for Factual Completeness

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-07-21 Benchmark · Long-form generation · Factuality · Multimodal · Wearable imagery · Rubric evaluation

Overview

  • Authors: Xilun Chen, Zhaleh Feizollahi, Ross Goodwin, Seungwhan Moon, Scott Yih, Pinar Donmez, Babak Damavandi, Luna Dong. Affiliation: AI at Meta (FAIR).
  • Central claim: long-form factuality evaluation has focused almost entirely on precision (are the model’s claims correct?) and neglected completeness (does the response contain everything it should?). Existing decompose-search-verify pipelines catch incorrect claims but cannot enumerate what a complete answer should have covered, especially when the required facts form open-ended sets, ordered processes, or relational structures rather than flat lists of independent boolean checks.
  • Contribution: a two-level meta-rubric framework, instantiated as GAMUT (Grounded Assessment of Multimodal factUalTy), with 1,813 questions grounded in real wearable imagery across 10 diverse domains, each paired with an evidence-backed rubric verified by expert human annotators. A modality-agnostic text-only variant is released alongside the multimodal benchmark.

Methodology

  • Two-level meta-rubric representation (named contribution). Problem. Flat checklist rubrics collapse structural information: they cannot express that some facts belong to open-ended coverage sets, that others must appear in a specific process order, or that certain fact clusters have relational dependencies. This means an LLM judge scoring a flat rubric may miss whole classes of completeness failures. Mechanism. Each question is annotated with a structured meta-rubric that captures the organization and relative importance of required content (open-ended set membership, process ordering, relational structure). At evaluation time, the structured meta-rubric is mechanically compiled into a flat checklist of binary, machine-gradable rubrics that an LLM judge can score reliably. Compilation is deterministic, so the same meta-rubric produces the same downstream checklist. Why. The two-level structure lets annotators express what a complete answer should look like without asking the LLM judge to do structural reasoning, and lets the LLM judge focus on the one thing it does well: yes/no verification of individual atomic claims. The paper argues this decoupling is what makes the benchmark robust to judge choice, and reports the ranking is stable across judges.
  • Grounded question construction. 1,813 questions grounded in real wearable-imagery evidence across 10 diverse domains. Each question is paired with an evidence-backed rubric (i.e., every required fact has a provenance in the grounding image), and expert human annotators verify the rubric. This is not synthetic-question construction: the wearable-imagery corpus provides the domain and the ground truth simultaneously, so the rubric can only include facts a model could in principle derive from the input.
  • Text-only variant. Because the meta-rubric framework is modality-agnostic, Meta releases a text-only companion benchmark alongside the multimodal one, allowing evaluation of text-only frontier models against the same completeness structure.

Evaluation & results

  • 14 frontier and open-weight models evaluated. The paper reports the benchmark is genuinely challenging: the top score is 58.7% from Gemini 3.1 Pro. That is far from saturation for a benchmark whose target property (factual completeness) is what production long-form workloads — report drafting, meeting summarization, document analysis — actually need.
  • Discriminative and judge-robust. The paper reports the benchmark is highly discriminative across the 14 models and robust to the choice of LLM judge — a direct answer to the primary methodological complaint against rubric-based benchmarks, which is that they inherit the biases of whichever judge model scores them.
  • Meta positions GAMUT as complementary to precision-focused factuality suites (FActScore-style decompose-verify pipelines): together, precision + completeness give a two-axis picture that neither alone does.

Other

  • Benchmark release, including the meta-rubrics and the text-only variant, are announced in the paper; the arXiv abstract does not specify a public code/data URL beyond the arXiv listing.

Stale but Stable: Staleness-Adaptive Trust Regions for Stabilizing Asynchronous Reinforcement Learning

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-07-21 Asynchronous RL · Trust region · PPO / GSPO · MoE routing · Reasoning post-training

Overview

  • Authors: Junyao Yang, Yucheng Shi, Zongxia Li, Zhongzhi Li, Ruhan Wang, Xiangxin Zhou, Kishan Panaganti, Haitao Mi, Leowei Liang. Affiliation: Tencent Hunyuan. Code: github.com/jyyang26/SAT. Blog: jyyang26.github.io/stable_async_analysis.
  • Central claim: asynchronous RL improves throughput by decoupling rollout generation from optimization, but staleness — a mismatch between the policy that generated a rollout and the current training policy — is inevitable and compounded by policy lag, engine delays, and MoE routing drift. PPO’s clipping only gates sampled outward updates and acts as a sampled surrogate rather than a full-policy trust-region constraint; in the asynchronous regime where staleness matters most, high-mismatch updates remain weakly controlled. The Staleness-Adaptive Trust Region (SAT) is a strictly-tighter alternative that contracts only the sign-selected endpoint of the nominal PPO interval on high-mismatch tokens, preserving baseline behavior on ordinary tokens.

Methodology

  • Staleness-Adaptive Trust Region (SAT, named contribution). Problem. From a trust-region perspective, PPO clipping bounds a sampled log-ratio and constrains only the outward direction of a sampled update; under asynchronous rollouts, high-staleness updates have an unbounded finite-horizon approximation error that PPO clipping does not gate. MoE routing drift is a specific instance: routes chosen under the rollout policy may not correspond to routes taken by the current policy, so the log-ratio proxy is noisier than in dense-model RL. Mechanism. SAT uses the detached sampled log-ratio as a practical staleness proxy: for each token in the batch, the log-ratio between rollout and current policy is measured without gradient flow. High-mismatch tails within each batch are identified via a staleness-based kernel scaling. On identified high-mismatch tokens, SAT contracts only the sign-selected endpoint of the nominal PPO interval — if the update direction is outward, the outward endpoint is pulled inward; the inward endpoint is left untouched. Ordinary-staleness tokens see the baseline PPO interval unchanged. The paper proves two properties: local interval containment (the SAT interval is a subset of the PPO interval) and pointwise pessimism relative to PPO (the SAT update magnitude is bounded above by the PPO update magnitude at every point). Why. Contracting only the sign-selected endpoint preserves the sample-efficiency advantage of PPO’s asymmetric clipping while restoring trust-region-like behavior on exactly the tokens where the sampled surrogate is weakest. The design is complementary to routing replay (R3, which replays MoE routing decisions from the rollout policy to reduce routing drift): SAT targets the mismatch tails, R3 targets the routing inconsistency, and the two combine multiplicatively in the reported results.
  • Experimental setup. Decoupled asynchronous RL setup on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, SGLang as inference engine, Megatron for training. SAT is composed with GSPO (a group-relative sequence policy optimization variant common in 2026 reasoning post-training) and with the R3 routing-replay stabilizer.

Evaluation & results

  • AIME24 avg@8 under decoupled asynchronous RL over Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base: SAT-GSPO + R3 achieves the best observed score, reaching 35.83 at lag 1 and 34.79 at lag 8. SAT-GSPO alone reaches 34.17 at lag 1. These are the highest reported AIME24 avg@8 numbers in the asynchronous regime at both low and high lag.
  • The lag-1 to lag-8 gap of only 1.04 points for SAT-GSPO+R3 is the key stability claim: standard GSPO shows a much larger degradation from lag 1 to lag 8 under the same setup (the paper reports adaptive clipping and routing replay act as complementary stabilizers targeting mismatch tails and routing inconsistency respectively).

Ablations

  • Ablating routing replay (R3) alone vs. SAT alone vs. SAT + R3 shows the two mechanisms address orthogonal failure modes: R3 alone stabilizes routing but does not fully control mismatch tails; SAT alone controls mismatch tails but does not fix routing drift; the combination gives the best AIME24 avg@8 at high lag.
  • The paper proves the SAT clipping interval is a strict subset of the PPO clipping interval and that the SAT update magnitude is pointwise bounded by the PPO update magnitude, giving a formal guarantee that SAT can only be more conservative than PPO on any given token.

Other

  • Positioned as a candidate replacement for scalar PPO / GSPO clipping in the asynchronous MoE-RL stacks powering recent 2026 Tier 1 launches like Ring-2.5-1T-Zero and Kimi K3, where staleness compounded by routing drift is a known bottleneck for scaling asynchronous rollouts to trillion-parameter MoE routers.
