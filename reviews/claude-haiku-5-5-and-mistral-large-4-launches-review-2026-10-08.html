TRACE: Rollout-Guided Quantization-Aware Training for FP4 Reinforcement Learning of MoE Language Models

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-10-06 FP4 RL · MoE · Quantization-aware training

Overview

  • Xin Wang, Hao Yu, Zhengyang Zhuge, Bochao Mao, Zheng Li, Junda Feng, Yuyan Luo, Yi Zhang, Yizhong Cao, Mi Zhang, Dayiheng Liu, Jianwei Zhang (Alibaba Qwen team per the submitter badge on Hugging Face Daily Papers) release TRACE, an FP4 reinforcement-learning framework for MoE language models. The paper's two targets are: (i) matching BF16-rollout quality on reasoning, coding, and long-horizon RL tasks while running the rollouts at FP4; and (ii) reducing storage and inter-node communication in the FP4 QAT loop by caching mantissa and scale information from deeper layers rather than recomputing them.
  • Positioning: existing FP4 RL methods optimize quantization on the training path and on the rollout path independently. The claim is that this leaves a quantization gap between the two paths that the policy never trains against, so the FP4 rollout quietly drifts from what the FP4-adapted policy expects.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Rollout-guided FP4 quantization-aware training.
    • Problem. FP4 has a tiny dynamic range per rounding bin, so even small differences in rounding decisions between training and rollout steps compound: the FP4-trained policy "wants" one rounding of the activations, the FP4 rollout engine produces another, and the on-policy gradient estimator receives a biased sample. Prior FP4 RL methods quantize the two paths independently and tolerate that bias.
    • Mechanism. TRACE conditions the FP4 rounding decisions used during training on the quantization outcomes observed on the rollout side. Concretely: the training-side quantizer takes as input the rounding bin that the FP4 rollout engine produced for the same activation (per layer, per expert), rather than recomputing from scratch, so the training policy's gradients are evaluated under the same FP4 noise distribution the rollout policy actually saw.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Prior FP4 QAT work (e.g., post-training quantization for inference) tolerates a small rounding gap because inference is non-interactive. In RL that gap biases the policy gradient. Closing the gap by shipping the rollout-side quantization outcomes back into the training-side quantizer removes that bias term; the paper reports FP4 rollout at parity with BF16 rollout on four MoE models and up to 5.4× rollout speedup.
  • Named contribution — Mantissa / scale caching for deeper layers.
    • Problem. In a granular MoE, the activations at deeper layers have higher sparsity and more stable per-expert distributions than those at shallower layers. Recomputing mantissa and scale metadata for every FP4 rollout step wastes storage and inter-node bandwidth because most of that metadata is unchanged step-to-step.
    • Mechanism. TRACE caches a selected subset of mantissa and scale values from deeper MoE layers and reuses them across rollout steps, only recomputing when a drift threshold is crossed.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. At deep MoE layers, the per-expert distribution of pre-quantization activations drifts slowly; the overhead of refreshing their FP4 metadata on every step is nearly pure waste. Caching those values gives most of the storage/communication saving with none of the quality cost. The paper reports storage and communication reductions on top of the quantization-gap closure.

Evaluation & results

  • Evaluated across four large-scale MoE language models (not named in the abstract) spanning reasoning, coding, and long-horizon RL tasks.
  • Headline: joint FP4 weight-and-activation plus FP4 KV-cache rollout performs comparably to BF16 rollout at matched RL training schedule.
  • Rollout speedup: up to 5.4× versus the BF16 rollout baseline, per the abstract.
  • Per-benchmark pass rates, model sizes, and ablation deltas are not in the abstract; those await the arXiv PDF.

Ablations

  • Not enumerated in the abstract-level release.

Other

  • Positioning: the paper describes the four MoE targets only as "large-scale MoE language models." Given the Qwen team affiliation, the natural guess is the Qwen3 and Qwen3-Omni families; the paper does not confirm this.

AdvSim2Real: Training Web Agents Against Adaptive Prompt Injection in a Web World Model

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-10-06 Prompt injection · Adversarial training · Web world models

Overview

  • Sarim Hashmi, Mukul Ranjan, Kshitij Mishra, Mikhail Kuznetsov, Praneeth Vepakomma, Nils Lukas (MBZUAI, inferred from the Sarim-MBZUAI code repository) present AdvSim2Real, a framework for training web agents to resist adaptive prompt injection by co-evolving a task curriculum, an injection adversary, and the agent itself inside a frozen web world model.
  • The framing problem: fixed-injection adversarial training generalizes poorly, and keeping the task distribution fixed means tasks lose learning signal once the agent solves them. Both axes need to co-evolve for the resulting robustness to transfer to a real browser.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Co-evolving curriculum, injection adversary, and agent in a frozen web world model.
    • Problem. Standard adversarial training against prompt injection uses a fixed set of injected instructions on a fixed task distribution. The resulting agent memorizes those injections, but an adaptive attacker who samples new injections at test time breaks it. If the training only augments the injection distribution and holds tasks fixed, tasks drop out of the gradient as soon as the agent solves them.
    • Mechanism. AdvSim2Real runs all three populations — task-curriculum generator, injection adversary, and agent — inside a frozen web world model (a learned simulator of browser interactions that stays fixed during training). The task curriculum keeps proposing new tasks and specifically targets tasks the current agent solves "about half the time," which is the maximal-gradient regime. The injection adversary is rewarded only when its injection flips a would-be success into a failure. The agent is trained end-to-end against both.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The frozen world model gives all three populations a reproducible playground, so the three can be optimized against each other without any party controlling the environment. The 50%-solve-rate target keeps every training batch at the information-maximum of task difficulty, and the injection adversary's "flip-a-success-into-a-failure" reward focuses on injections that are actually dangerous rather than injections that look sneaky but never land. Prior adversarial-training work either held tasks fixed (so gradient dies) or held the injection distribution fixed (so attacks don't adapt).

Evaluation & results

  • Headline: a 4B-parameter agent trained with AdvSim2Real is "both more capable and more robust" than the base agent across 150 web tasks.
  • Completion rate under an unseen frontier-model injection adversary rises +33.6% relative over the base agent.
  • Robustness transfers from the frozen web world model to a real browser (the paper reports gains "transfer to a real browser"; specific real-browser pass-rate numbers are not in the abstract).

Ablations

  • Not enumerated at abstract level. The three natural ablations — freeze each of the three populations in turn — await the PDF.

Other

  • Code is at a Sarim-MBZUAI GitHub repository (linked from the arXiv page), which is also what satisfies the frontier-lab affiliation signal for this entry pending explicit affiliations in the PDF.

NVIDIA

NeMo-DCR: Bit-Exact Delta-Compressed Refit for Scalable Agentic RL at Trillion-Parameter Scale

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-10-06 Agentic RL · Weight synchronization · Trillion-parameter systems

Overview

  • Songlin Jiang, Zhiyu Li, Terry Kong, Yu Yao, Youngeun Kwon, Bernard Nguyen, Ashwath Aithal, Mario Di Francesco at NVIDIA (affiliation inferred from the open-source landing in NVIDIA NeMo RL pull request #2444) release NeMo-DCR, a framework for synchronizing updated policy weights to agentic-RL rollout clusters at trillion-parameter scale without any quality compromise.
  • The scaling problem the paper targets: in agentic RL, trained weights must land on rollout clusters before the next batch starts; at 1T parameters a full checkpoint transfer between two AWS regions takes 87.5 minutes, which dominates wall-clock. Prior lossy compression methods trade bit-exactness for speed, which is unacceptable when rollout and training must remain in exact sync for the on-policy gradient estimator.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Bit-exact delta-compressed refit via affine projection and residual XOR masks.
    • Problem. Only about 1% of BF16 weight values actually change per RL step, but the sharding and ordering between the training cluster's shards and the rollout cluster's shards don't line up, so a naive diff plus patch produces a non-exact result.
    • Mechanism. NeMo-DCR runs in two passes. First, an affine projection converts training-shard changes into the rollout cluster's canonical checkpoint coordinates. Second, a residual conversion handles whatever changes do not map affinely. Both passes emit either a compressible XOR mask (for affine-projected changes) or an overwrite (for residuals); both are applied in place on the receiver and are therefore bit-identical to a dense refit.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The affine projection step captures the vast majority of changes with tiny XOR masks; the residual conversion handles the tail cheaply via overwrites. Prior refit systems either shipped the full checkpoint (slow but exact) or used lossy compression (fast but not bit-exact and so unusable for on-policy RL). NeMo-DCR gets both endpoints: 12–40× speedup over the transport-only reference while remaining bit-identical to the full-checkpoint result.
  • Named contribution — Retry-safe in-place application with joint commit.
    • Problem. When one node in a rollout cluster fails midway through writing a delta, the partially-written weights are now in an inconsistent state between policy and baseline, and the next training step is invalid.
    • Mechanism. The in-place application is retry-safe: if a write fails, the writer retries from a safe checkpoint. A joint commit step binds the policy to its baseline atomically; neither is visible to the rollout engine until both are fully written.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. On-policy RL requires that policy and baseline stay perfectly aligned, else the advantage estimator is biased by the mismatch between the policy that generated the trajectory and the baseline against which the trajectory is scored. The joint commit guarantees this alignment is never partially broken by a failed write.
  • Named contribution — Object-storage and relay-tree streaming (no cross-cluster collective).
    • Problem. A cross-cluster all-reduce or all-gather for a 1T-parameter refit is slow and brittle: it requires synchronous participation of every rank in both clusters, and a single stalled rank blocks the entire operation.
    • Mechanism. NeMo-DCR streams the compressed delta over either an object-storage drop (write once from the trainer, read many times from the rollouts) or a relay tree (training-cluster root pushes deltas down a BFS tree of relay nodes that fan out to the rollout cluster). Neither path requires a global synchronous collective.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Removing the cross-cluster collective means a stalled rollout node no longer blocks the trainer; the trainer can proceed with the next step while the stalled rollout node catches up via the object-storage drop or a retry on the relay tree. The paper reports a 1T relay-tree refit at 3% change in 150 seconds, versus 87.5 minutes for a transport-only full-checkpoint reference between the same two AWS regions.

Evaluation & results

  • Headline: full 1T checkpoint transfer between two AWS regions takes 87.5 minutes; a 1T relay-tree delta refit at 3% change takes 150 seconds.
  • Change rates tested: 3% and 5% per step (the paper also reports approximately 1% of BF16 weight values store changed values per step as the baseline rate).
  • Model-size coverage: 30B to 1T parameters.
  • Speedup: 12–40× over a transport-only full-checkpoint reference.
  • Bit-exactness: the delta refit's result is bit-identical to a full dense refit by construction; the paper's framing is that this is non-negotiable for on-policy RL.

Ablations

  • Not fully enumerated at abstract level, but the two obvious ablation axes are visible: delta compression (XOR masks vs. overwrites per class of change) and transport layer (relay tree vs. object storage).

Other

  • License: CC BY 4.0. Code is open-sourced in NVIDIA NeMo RL pull request #2444. The DOI is listed as pending registration.
  • Positioning: this is the first bit-exact delta-refit system published at 1T-parameter scale for RL training; prior systems were either at smaller scale (<100B) or lossy. The dense-refit baseline it compares against is a wire-level transport baseline, not a prior-art compression baseline.

UNREAL: Unifying Retrieval and Long-Context with a Single Model

Tier 2 · Research Paper arXiv 2026-10-06 Retrieval · Long context · Frozen-backbone adapters

Overview

  • Edan Kinderman, Elad Hoffer, Yochai Blau, Brian Chmiel, Ron Banner, Daniel Soudry, Boris Ginsburg (NVIDIA, inferred from Boris Ginsburg's NVIDIA affiliation and the paper's engineering style) propose UNREAL, a single-model framework that unifies corpus retrieval and long-context inference by selecting evidence from a frozen LLM's own internal representations.
  • The split that UNREAL closes: retrieval today uses a dedicated retriever/reranker stack whose embedding geometry is uncoupled from the LLM that eventually reads the retrieved chunks, and long-context inference drowns the LLM in retrieved distractors because the retriever's "relevance" signal doesn't map onto the reader's own attention. UNREAL uses one representation space for both.

Methodology

  • Named contribution — Shared frozen-backbone retrieval and inference.
    • Problem. A retriever that is trained separately from the reader LLM optimizes a different objective (lexical/semantic relevance) than what the reader actually needs (evidence that reduces its own loss on the downstream task). Switching to the reader's representation space usually requires finetuning the backbone, which is expensive and destroys the reader's existing capability.
    • Mechanism. UNREAL encodes chunks and constructs retrieval queries directly from the frozen LLM's internal representations, with fewer than 500K trainable parameters added as adapters on top. The backbone is not modified, so its downstream reading capability is preserved. The adapters learn to project the frozen representations into a retrieval-friendly geometry.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. Because retrieval and inference share the frozen backbone's representation, "relevant to the retriever" and "relevant to the reader" are the same signal by construction. Adding fewer than 500K trainable parameters is small enough to train on a single-GPU budget and small enough to not perturb the base model's capabilities. The paper reports that this single-model stack beats dedicated retriever-reranker systems on multi-hop retrieval recall.
  • Named contribution — Distractor removal for long-context inference.
    • Problem. Long-context benchmarks (NoLiMa, LV-Eval) show that LLMs degrade sharply when the context contains distractors, even when the correct evidence is within context. The reader's attention smears across the irrelevant chunks and the signal from the correct chunk gets diluted.
    • Mechanism. At inference time, UNREAL uses the same retrieval mechanism to pre-filter the long context, removing distractor chunks before the reader generates. Because the retriever runs on the frozen backbone, this pre-filter is extremely cheap and shares all of the reader's prior.
    • Why it works / why it beats prior. The reader's downstream loss is dominated by distractor-driven attention smearing; removing distractors moves the reader back to the clean-context regime it already handles well. The paper reports NoLiMa accuracy at 128K rising from 1.0% to 24.83%.

Evaluation & results

  • Retrieval, on a 3B-token / 21M-chunk Wikipedia index. All four UNREAL dense/hybrid backbones reportedly outperform SOTA retriever-reranker systems. Best-model recall:
    BenchmarkPrior SOTAUNREAL
    HotpotQA49.1%73.2%
    2WikiMultiHopQA31.7%60.1%
    MuSiQue8.8%14.4%
  • Long context (distractor removal before generation):
    • NoLiMa at 128K: 1.0% → 24.83%.
    • LV-Eval F1 at 256K: 49.97% → 54.66%.
  • Efficiency: versus full-context inference, UNREAL cuts FLOPs and time-to-first-token (TTFT) from roughly 32K tokens of context onward; the FLOP/TTFT gap widens as context length grows.

Ablations

  • Not enumerated at abstract level. The implied ablation set is the per-task sweep across dense vs. hybrid UNREAL backbones (four variants reported) and across context-length budgets.

Other

  • Trainable-parameter budget of <500K is the signature engineering claim: the retrieval capability is added on top of a frozen LLM without touching the backbone, which means UNREAL slots in behind any LLM that preserves its intermediate representations.

Sharing AI Progress in Mathematics: 722 Manuscripts from an Unreleased Internal Frontier Model

Tier 3 · Blog Post + Research Artifact Release openai.com 2026-10-06 Math reasoning · Lean formalization · Unreleased model

Overview

  • OpenAI publishes a research-math release at openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics and github.com/openai/math, dated 2026-10-06: 722 mathematical manuscripts organized into 372 families, generated by an unnamed unreleased internal frontier model. Many results carry Lean formalizations; many do not, and the README cautions that unformalized results "could have issues."
  • Model identity: not disclosed. External coverage describes it as "an internal frontier model" that is "significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra." The blog post does not benchmark the model, name it, or share weights; OpenAI says it is "working to responsibly release the model." The 2026-09-29 Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (AGMAI) at the Institute for Advanced Study published a set of recommendations the day before, saying labs should stop testing advanced math problems on proprietary models the wider community cannot access — this release is OpenAI's explicit response.
  • Named reasoning summaries in the README cover 10 representative families:
    • 007 — Ordinary two-point correlations of multiplicative functions
    • 017 — The irrationality exponent of π
    • 087 — Symmetric and general Mahler conjectures
    • 102 — Ordinary NP-hardness at the basic semidefinite threshold
    • 159 — Quasipolynomial bounds for arithmetic progressions
    • 197 — Kaplansky's direct-finiteness conjecture in characteristic two
    • 221 — The Mézard–Parisi formula for diluted spin glasses
    • 271 — Spontaneous magnetization in the quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet
    • 287 — Isomorphism of free group factors
    • 362 — The three-dimensional relativistic Vlasov–Maxwell system
  • Named theorems outside the 10-family summary:
    • A zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function; the included write-up covers Re(s) > 11/12, and OpenAI notes it was human-edited for readability.
    • A proof of the Hodge Conjecture for CM abelian varieties.
    • The release follows the 2026-09-08 OpenAI announcement of a Lean-formalized proof that smooth 3D Navier–Stokes flow can develop a finite-time singularity (training began 2026-08-28; evaluation started 2026-09-01; the agents reached the result on 2026-09-05). OpenAI does not plan to claim the Millennium Prize.
  • Methodology: the model was posed approximately 4,000 problems using a fixed procedure; outputs were aggregated into families and manuscripts under a significance threshold. Average compute per result: approximately 3 hours of ChatGPT Pro-equivalent thinking. Named exceptions departed from the fixed procedure, notably the Re(s) > 11/12 zeta write-up and the Hodge CM-abelian-varieties proof.
  • Governance: OpenAI consulted the Institute for Advanced Study's AGMAI, published a revision/citation protocol in the repository, and will keep adding Lean formalizations and corrections (with earlier versions kept accessible). OpenAI also commits to funding workshops, conferences, and special programs on verifying AI-produced major results.

Other

  • Why Tier 3 and not Tier 1: the model itself is unreleased — no weights, no API, no system card, no serving endpoint. The artifact is the mathematical output plus the policy framing. If the model does ship later, that release would become its own Tier 1 entry; this entry documents the research artifact that landed in-window.
  • The release is the first time a frontier lab has published hundreds of research-math manuscripts from a model whose weights never shipped, and the first large-scale demonstration of Lean formalization at research-math scale from a public lab.
